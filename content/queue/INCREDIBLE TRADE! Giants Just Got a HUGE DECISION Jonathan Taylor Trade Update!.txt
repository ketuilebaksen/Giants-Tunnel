The Jonathan Taylor trade market just got real, and the Giants are right in the middle of it. Adam Schefter and Ian Rapoport have both listed the Giants among the teams interested in the Colts’ star running back. Tom Pelissero reported that the Colts turned down an offer of a 2027 second-round pick plus another pick for Taylor. We do not know which team made that offer. But think about what it tells you. A second-round pick and more was not enough. And that is not even the biggest decision the Giants have made this deadline season. Because before Taylor, there was Jeffery Simmons. According to Jonathan Jones, Joe Schoen tried to get somewhere with Tennessee on the All-Pro defensive tackle. When the talks stalled, John Harbaugh reportedly picked up the phone and called Titans owner Amy Adams Strunk himself. And then the Giants walked away, and Chicago paid a massive price instead. So today, the question is simple. If the Giants were willing to walk away from Simmons, what are they willing to pay for Taylor? Let’s get into it.

If the Colts said yes to a first-round pick straight up for Jonathan Taylor, would you make the trade?

Let me start with the Simmons story, because I think it tells you more about this front office than anything else this season. Jeffery Simmons is one of the best interior defensive linemen in football. The Bears ended up acquiring him from Tennessee, and the reported package was enormous. Chicago sent its 2027 first and fourth-round picks and its 2028 first-round pick for Simmons and a 2028 fifth. Two first-round picks. That is the price of an All-Pro defensive tackle in the middle of a season. And remember, the Bears made the divisional round last year. They are a team that believes its window is open right now, so I understand why they paid it.

But look at what the Giants did. Schoen made the call. When it stalled, the head coach called the owner directly. That is aggressive. That is a franchise saying we are serious. And then, when the price went where it went, the Giants let Chicago pay it. That is the part I love. It is easy to be aggressive. It is hard to be aggressive and disciplined at the same time. Harbaugh got the owner on the phone and still had the sense to walk away. That tells you the Giants are not going to buy just to buy.

And it lines up perfectly with what Schoen said during the bye. It has got to make sense short term and long term. If a team is asking too much, and it does not match up with where you see the value, it does not make sense. The Simmons situation was that answer in action. Now the Taylor situation is the next test.

And I want to give credit where it is due. The relationship between Schoen and Harbaugh is clearly working. A general manager who is comfortable letting his head coach call an owner directly is a general manager who trusts his coach. That is not how it always works in the NFL. A lot of front offices would see that as a turf war. Here, it looks like teamwork. And we have already seen this partnership deliver. They traded a 2027 fifth-round pick to Minnesota for J.J. McCarthy, and in his first start for the Giants, he won on the road in overtime with zero turnovers.

Now, let’s get to Taylor. What do we actually know? Schefter and Rapoport both have the Giants among the interested teams. The Colts are listening. And Pelissero reported that an offer of a 2027 second plus another pick was turned down. We do not know the team, and we do not know what round the other pick was in. And Taylor himself has said he wants to stay in Indianapolis. He said, hopefully that day may not come, but he cannot control that. That is a player who knows the business, and who knows the phone is ringing.

So, what does that rejected offer tell us? It tells us the price is at least a second-round pick plus something more. Maybe a lot more. Earlier in this deadline season, there was a lot of talk that the Colts would want a first-round pick for Taylor. That rejected offer makes that talk look a lot more realistic.

And here is where I have to give you a receipt. A few videos ago, I told you I would be stunned if the Giants gave up a first-round pick for a running back in the middle of a season. I still believe that is the most likely outcome. But I have to be fair. The board has changed since I said that. National insiders now have the Giants on the list of interested teams. And the Simmons story tells us this front office is willing to make big calls. So I am not taking back my prediction yet. But I will say this. If the Giants do send a first for Taylor, I will be the first one on this channel to tell you I was wrong.

Now, let’s talk about the case for Taylor. And the best case is not about this week. It is about what happens when a defense takes the run away. Picture Philadelphia on November 8th. The Eagles load the box early. The first few runs go nowhere. It is second and eight. Do you hand it off again? Or do you put the game on the shoulders of a quarterback making his second start for the Giants? With Taylor, you would feel a lot better about handing it off again. That is what a great running back gives you. The confidence to stay with your plan when the plan is struggling.

And the coaching staff is built to use two backs. Matt Nagy is working with McCarthy. Greg Roman is on the staff, and Roman built some of the best rushing attacks of the last decade with Harbaugh in Baltimore. Dennard Wilson has been creative on defense with a rookie linebacker. This is a staff that knows how to use talent in different ways. Give that staff Taylor and Skattebo together, two backs a defense has to account for on every snap, and I think they could build something really dangerous.

And think about what Taylor would do for Malik Nabers. If a defense keeps a safety deep to help on Nabers, you hand the ball to Taylor against a lighter box. If the safety comes down to stop Taylor, McCarthy looks at Nabers one-on-one. That is the kind of conflict that wins playoff games. And we just watched an example of it. The Seattle Seahawks won the last Super Bowl with Sam Darnold making the throws he needed to make and Kenneth Walker the Third running his way to Super Bowl MVP. A great run game and a good quarterback can take you a long way.

And let me remind you who Taylor is, because this is not some average back we are talking about. In 2021, he led the entire NFL with 1,811 rushing yards and 18 rushing touchdowns. He has the rare combination of size, vision, and breakaway speed that turns a four-yard run into a 60-yard touchdown. He is the kind of back who changes how a defensive coordinator calls a game. And Giants fans know better than anyone what a great running back can do for a team. The Giants let Saquon Barkley walk in 2024. He went to Philadelphia, ran for over 2,000 yards that season, and won a Super Bowl. Nobody in this fan base needs a lecture about the difference an elite back can make. We watched it happen, in green, every week.

And there is a contract question that matters too. Before the Giants send anything to Indianapolis, Schoen has to know exactly how long Taylor would be a Giant and what he would cost after this season. A player who helps for ten games and leaves in March is worth a lot less than a player who helps for two or three playoff runs. That is one detail I will be watching closely if this gets serious.

But now let me give you the case against Taylor. And it starts with Houston. We just watched our own backs win a road game in overtime. Cam Skattebo broke off a 42-yard run. On the very next snap, Devin Singletary took it 30 yards for the walk-off touchdown. Houston never got the ball in overtime. The Giants ran for 188 yards in that game, with four rushing touchdowns from four different players. So why are we talking ourselves into a premium pick for another back?

And there is a culture piece too. Remember what happened against the Saints. Skattebo played only a handful of offensive snaps and did not get a single carry, after his overtime fumble in Washington. Harbaugh drew a line. And then Skattebo answered it with the best game of his career, 17 carries for 124 yards and a touchdown. If you are a coach who tells young players they can earn more work, you have to mean it when one of them comes through. Bringing in a star back right after Skattebo’s breakout sends a complicated message. I am not saying it is the wrong message. I am saying the coaches would have to handle it carefully.

And if Taylor comes, what happens to everybody else? Tyrone Tracy Jr. is on injured reserve with a hamstring and will miss at least four games. For now, Singletary would slide into the third role behind Taylor and Skattebo. But once Tracy is healthy, he deserves touches too. Somebody gets squeezed. I know that sounds cold right after Singletary scored a walk-off touchdown. But being glad a player is on your team and wanting him to get 20 carries in Philadelphia are two different conversations.

And then there is the part of the case against Taylor that worries me the most. The draft picks. Look at what the Giants just got from their recent draft classes. Arvell Reese, a 21-year-old linebacker who rushes the passer one series and takes away a throwing lane the next. Francis Mauigoa, who looks really good moving in the run game, and whose pass protection deserves time to develop. Malachi Fields, who gave McCarthy a 47-yard bomb in Houston. Draft picks are not just numbers. They are players like that. And when you trade a first, you are trading the chance to find the next one.

And it takes time to know what you have. Think about Deonte Banks. Joe Schoen admitted during the bye that Banks had a great rookie year, then struggled for a couple of years, and only now looks like the corner the Giants hoped for. Think about Andrew Thomas, who had a rough start before becoming one of the best left tackles in football. Picks are valuable because they give you a chance. But that chance takes years to pay off.

Now, here is something else to keep in mind. The Giants are short on picks this year already. Their 2027 fourth went in the deal to move up for Malachi Fields in the third round. Their 2027 fifth went to Minnesota in the McCarthy trade. They still have their first, second, and third-round picks, plus two sixths and a projected compensatory fourth. That is why Schoen said a third might be too rich and a sixth might not be enough. Every pick matters more when you have fewer of them.

And there is one more thing I keep thinking about. The value of a first-round pick changes depending on where it lands. If the Giants win the division and play into January, that pick probably lands somewhere in the 20s. That is a reasonable price for a player like Taylor. But if the tougher half of the schedule catches up with the Giants, that pick could land a lot earlier. That is the gamble. And remember, even if Taylor plays great, a running back can do his job and you can still lose. One player does not guarantee anything.

Now, let’s talk about competition, because the Giants are not the only team calling. A lot of the speculation has focused on the Minnesota Vikings. And yes, that is the same team that traded McCarthy to the Giants. Minnesota has been one of the best teams in the league this season, built around a strong defense. You can see exactly why the Vikings would want Taylor. Give that defense a lead, then hand Taylor the ball and run out the clock. That is a championship formula.

And here is the tricky part for the Giants. If both teams offered the same picks, you would think the Colts might prefer the Giants’ picks, because the Vikings have the better record, so their picks would probably land later. But all it takes is one extra pick from Minnesota, and that advantage disappears. In a bidding war, the team with the deepest pick cupboard usually wins. And right now, the Giants are short a fourth and a fifth.

So, is there a version of a Taylor trade I would actually like? Yes. Art Stapleton, who covers the Giants, floated an idea. And I want to be clear, this is his proposal, not a report. In his idea, the Giants would get Taylor and defensive tackle Grover Stewart from the Colts. The Giants would send defensive tackle Darius Alexander, their 2027 second-round pick, and a 2028 second-round pick that could become a first.

What I like about that idea is that it brings back two players who could play meaningful snaps right away. Picture Stewart inside when Philadelphia wants to run the ball on third and short. With Dexter Lawrence gone, the Giants need a big body in the middle, and Stewart is exactly that. And it fits what I said in my last video. The Giants have plenty of speed off the edge. What they need is size and strength inside. That is a deal where two players help the 2026 Giants, not just one.

But if you take Stewart out of that deal, I start asking questions. Why would the Giants send a young defensive lineman like Alexander to Indianapolis if they are not getting a defensive lineman back? The deal only makes sense as a package.

And of course, the Giants do not have to get everything from the Colts. They can shop for these jobs separately. Remember, San Francisco got DeForest Buckner from Indianapolis for a 2027 fourth-round pick. That is a reasonable price for a veteran defensive lineman. And at running back, there are other options. Tony Pollard, Tyler Allgeier, and Brian Robinson Jr. are the kind of names that come up. Having a backup plan at running back makes it a lot easier to hang up the phone when the Colts’ price gets too high. That is how good negotiators work. You never let the other side think you have no alternative.

So, here is where I land, and this is my opinion, not a report. For Taylor alone, I would go up to one first-round pick, and I would stop there. I would listen on two seconds. If the Colts ask for a first, a second, and a third, I hang up. If the Colts want Alexander in the deal, I want Stewart coming back. And if none of that works, I call about a cheaper veteran back, I protect my picks, and I trust Skattebo and Singletary until Tracy returns.

And here is the thing about timing. The deadline is November 10th, but the Giants play in Philadelphia on November 8th. If a deal happens before the Eagles game, Taylor would have a few days in the building, at best. If it happens after, the Giants would have one more data point on their own backfield. I think the bye week was the ideal time to get a deal done, so a new player could learn the offense before Philadelphia. That window is closing. So if you see news this week, that is why.

And let me say one more thing about the bigger picture. Even without Taylor, look at where this team is. When Jaxson Dart went down on the opening drive of week two against the Rams, a lot of fans thought four wins might be the total for the whole year. Instead, the Giants have four wins at the bye. They are 4 and 2 and 1, leading the NFC East, with Brian Burns and Tremaine Edmunds on injured reserve. I would give the first half a B-plus. I still want the Saints game back. I am still annoyed about the tie in Washington. But for the first time in a long time, the Giants are spending a bye week figuring out how to protect a division lead.

And that is why coaching matters so much. For years, one injury could change everything about what this team could do on a Sunday. Now, a rookie linebacker is playing in coverage and rushing the passer. A quarterback who showed up in late September is winning on the road. And a coach is calling owners directly, then walking away when the price is wrong. That is a different franchise.

So, here is what I will be watching. Number one, whether the Colts actually move Taylor before November 10th, and where he goes. Number two, whether the Giants pivot to a defensive tackle if Taylor’s price gets too high. Number three, how Skattebo’s hamstring looks coming out of the bye. Number four, whether the Vikings make a bigger push. And number five, Joe Schoen’s phone, because if he walked away from Simmons, he can walk away from anybody.

I have said it all season, and I am saying it again. This team can go all the way. With Taylor or without him.
