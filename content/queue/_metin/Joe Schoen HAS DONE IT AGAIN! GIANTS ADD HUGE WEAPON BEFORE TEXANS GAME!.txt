Joe Schoen did not need to make a single phone call this week to hand John Harbaugh new weapons for Houston. They were already in the building, and the Saints game just revealed them. A quarterback Schoen landed for a fifth-round pick put up 243 yards in one quarter. A day-three rookie tackle Schoen drafted stepped in on the blind side late in the fourth quarter, with the Saints pinning their ears back, and did not allow a single pressure. And a running back Schoen took in the fifth round a couple of years ago scored two touchdowns and graded out as the best pass protector on the entire team. Folks, everybody walked away from that loss talking about what went wrong. But when you go back through the tape, you find a front office that has quietly stocked this roster with real answers, at exactly the positions where the Giants need them most heading into the Texans game. Today, I am going to break down every one of those weapons, the numbers behind them, and a few names on defense that deserve a lot more attention than this loss is giving them. Let’s get into it.

Let me be honest first, because I do not believe in moral victories. The Giants have not won a game in more than two weeks. They tied a one-win Washington team and then lost at home to the Saints. At 3 and 2 and 1, in a division that is falling apart around them, they are not taking advantage the way they should. That frustrates me, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But frustration and evidence are two different things. And the evidence from Sunday says this roster has more pieces than the scoreboard showed.

Let’s start with the biggest one, J.J. McCarthy. Early in that game, the Giants looked flat. After the fumble before halftime and the Saints’ opening drive of the third quarter, it honestly looked like a team that was feeling sorry for itself. It was 34 to 10. People were heading for the exits at MetLife. And then McCarthy got inserted, and the whole energy of the football game changed. That is not me being dramatic. The offense started moving, and the Giants put up 17 unanswered points.

Now, I know what some people will say. Garbage time. And I have said it myself on this channel, the Saints were protecting a big lead, and defenses play differently in that spot. But here is the other side of it. McCarthy did not come in against a defense that was sitting back and relaxing. He came in when the Saints were pinning their ears back and getting after the quarterback, because they knew the Giants had to throw on every down. That is one of the hardest situations in football to play quarterback in, and he moved the ball anyway.

And think about the price. Schoen traded with the Vikings on September 28th, reportedly giving up a fifth-round pick, after Jackson Dart went down with his knee injury. At the time, it looked like a depth move, an insurance policy. Now look at it. We know what Jameis Winston is at this point. There is no mystery there. He is a gunslinger who will give you big plays and big mistakes. McCarthy is the unknown, and after Sunday, that unknown looks a lot more exciting. I am not saying hand him the keys and crown him. I am saying Schoen may have found a quarterback you can rely on this season, a quarterback who can keep you in contention for the NFC East. And for a fifth-round pick, that is what we call doing it again.

And folks, let’s talk about what contention means in this division. With the Eagles losing Jalen Hurts for the season and the Cowboys sitting at 2 and 4, it might only take eight, or dare I say nine, wins to take the NFC East. Somebody even joked about the Giants winning the division at 8 and 8 and 1. It sounds ridiculous, but with a division this unpredictable, I cannot completely rule it out. And that is exactly why having two quarterbacks who can win games matters. In a race this tight, one bad stretch from your starter can cost you the division, and a ready backup can save it.

Now, the second weapon, and honestly, he is the one who surprised me the most. J.C. Davis. This is your rookie left tackle, a late-round, day-three pick, and he had to fill in for Andrew Thomas for the final 25 snaps of the game. Think about that situation for a second. You are a rookie. It is late in the fourth quarter. The Saints know you have to throw, and they are coming after the quarterback on every snap. And now you are protecting the blind side. That is about as hard a spot as you can ask a young player to step into.

And he looked really, really good. Here are the numbers. Davis finished with a PFF grade of 70.5, the second-highest on the entire offensive line, right behind Andrew Thomas. His pass-blocking grade was 78.8, again second only to Thomas. He did not allow a single pressure in those 25 snaps, so his pass-blocking efficiency was a perfect 100 percent. And his run-blocking grade of 58.8 was also second on the line behind Thomas. So, when I say J.C. Davis was the Giants’ second-best offensive lineman in that game, in only 25 snaps, that is literally what the numbers say.

To me, you are looking at a guy who can be a real backup left tackle right now, maybe a player who can slide inside to left guard down the road, and at minimum a real asset on this line. And for a day-three pick, I will take that every single day. Most late-round rookies spend their first season on the inactive list, waiting for a chance that may never come. Giants fans, you know this story. Go back to David Diehl, a fifth-round pick in 2003. He started right away as a rookie, and he became the kind of lineman who could move between guard and tackle wherever the team needed him. In the 2007 season, he was the Giants’ starting left tackle on a team that won the Super Bowl. Late-round linemen with versatility and toughness are how championship lines get built. I am not saying Davis is Diehl after 25 snaps. I am saying that is the kind of player you hope a pick like this becomes.

Now, I want to be fair about the context, because this offensive line has not been great this season. There is no other way to dress that up. Andrew Thomas has been playing through an injury, and he is still playing at a high level, but it is not his usual shutdown self. Jon Runyan Jr. has been good in pass protection, but he has been one of the worst run blockers in the league. Jermaine Eluemunor has been really good in pass protection, but he did not play in this one. So, when you have a line that has been shaky, a rookie coming in and giving you that kind of performance is exactly what you want to see. And with Thomas’s health still a question heading into Houston, Davis might be more than depth. He might be the guy protecting the quarterback against the Texans’ pass rush. And honestly, after what he showed on Sunday, the idea of a rookie starting on the road in Houston does not scare me nearly as much as it would have a week ago.

Now, the third weapon, and pass protection is a big part of his story too. Tyrone Tracy Jr. Look, whether Harbaugh wants to call it a benching or not, Cam Skattebo did not get a carry against the Saints. And Harbaugh has made it clear that he is waiting, almost begging, for one running back to step up and take this job. So when Tracy shows up and has the game he had, you have to pay attention.

On paper, the numbers do not look great. Six carries for 18 yards. I will admit that. But he had two total touchdowns, and he flashed a lot of the things he does well. First, the receiving touchdown. Jameis Winston found him out of the backfield, and I loved the way he caught the ball in the flat, got north and south, and beat the Saints defensive backs to the pylon on that 17-yard catch and run. That was the first touchdown of the game. Then, the goal-line touchdown in the fourth quarter. The Giants were down 34 to 10, it was third and two from the five, and he got skinny, attacked the hole, showed some contact balance, and got downhill again. That touchdown started the comeback.

But here is what really got my attention. His pass protection. Tracy had an 80.8 PFF pass-blocking grade in this game, and that was number one on the entire Giants roster. Number one. And remember, pass protection was one of the big reasons he lost his role coming out of the preseason. So for him to come back and stand tall in blitz pickup, against a Saints defense that was sending pressure, that is a big deal. He showed you the pass catching. He showed you a nose for the end zone. And he showed you he can protect. And folks, think about how much that matters with a young quarterback like McCarthy potentially playing more. A back who can pick up the blitz buys the quarterback time to throw downfield, and we already know McCarthy wants to attack downfield.

And here is the Schoen part of it. Tracy was a fifth-round pick in 2024. A converted receiver who turned himself into a running back. Giants history is full of late-round backs who became heroes. Ahmad Bradshaw was a seventh-round pick in 2007, and he ended up scoring the go-ahead touchdown in Super Bowl XLVI. I am not putting Tracy in that category yet. But maybe a fan favorite is about to be back in the middle of this rushing attack. And if he keeps playing like this, he might be the answer to the question Harbaugh keeps asking.

Now, I do not want anybody to read this as me writing off Cam Skattebo, because I am not. Skattebo is still one of the toughest runners on this roster, and the energy he brings is real. He owned his mistakes, and he connected his reduced role to the fumble in Washington himself. That tells me he understands exactly what he has to fix. Harbaugh has been clear that this is an open competition, and when one back steps up, that back gets the job. Tracy just made his case. Now it is on Skattebo to answer in practice this week. Honestly, that is the best thing that can happen to this backfield. Competition brings out the best in players, and the Giants need the best version of somebody in that room by Sunday.

Now, let me zoom out for a second, because looking at these pieces makes me think about the future too. When Jackson Dart comes back as the starting quarterback next season, he is going to be throwing to some really good players. Malik Nabers is special. Isaiah Likely is special, and I think he has become one of the best tight ends in the league. Add one more playmaker, another receiver or an explosive back, and this offense could be one of the best in football. And now you can add a backup quarterback who has shown he can win games, a young tackle who can protect, and a running back who can catch, score, and block. That is what roster building looks like.

Now, let me flip to the defense, because there are some names over there I have been wanting to talk about. First, D.J. Reader. I know the run defense has been a problem this season. We saw it against the Saints, with 175 rushing yards allowed. But Reader has been a monster in the middle, and he was really good on Sunday too. Seven total tackles and seven defensive stops. And a stop, if you are not familiar with the term, is a play that counts as a failure for the offense.

Here is the problem, though. When Reader is on the field this season, the Giants are allowing around 4.5 yards per carry. When he is off the field, they are allowing 5.2. That is seven-tenths of a yard per carry, and over a full game, that difference adds up to first downs, field position, and points. But it cannot be one interior lineman carrying the whole thing. We learned that the hard way after Dexter Lawrence left this building. Somebody else in that defensive tackle room has to step up alongside Reader, whether it is Shelby Harris or Darius Alexander, who the coaches are still giving chances to be that guy. Because if one man is carrying the run defense, it is not sustainable, especially against a Houston team that will test the middle.

Then in the back end, I really liked how Ar’Darius Washington played. It was so nice to see a safety who was physical, fast, and assertive. He was shooting gaps, playing downfield, and a couple of times he looked like he got shot out of a cannon. He made some really nice open-field tackles too. Four tackles, and every single one of them counted as a stop. He also had an 81.4 PFF grade, the second-highest of any Giants defender in that game. With Tyler Nubin dealing with his neck injury, that is a safety making his case for more snaps.

And the last defensive name I want to get to is Kayvon Thibodeaux, because this is something people will miss completely. Two games in a row now, there have been no splash plays. No sacks. The pressures have not been obvious. So, if you are just watching casually, you might think he is not doing much. But his pass-rush win rate was 20 percent in this game, the highest of anybody on the Giants. And if you put weeks five and six together, Thibodeaux has the highest pass-rush win rate on the entire team, and that includes Abdul Carter. Remember, Harbaugh said a couple of weeks ago that people underestimate what Thibodeaux brings. The underlying numbers back him up. The sacks will come if he keeps winning at that rate.

So, when you put all of this together, I come back to where I started. This team did not quit. It was 34 to 10, the stadium was emptying out, and a lot of teams would have thrown in the towel and started thinking about the showers. Instead, the Giants fought their way back and were right in it until the very last play. That is the second week in a row they have done that. I do not take that for granted.

But I also want to be clear about what this means for Houston. Fighting back is not enough. The Giants need to start fast, protect the football, and stop the run. And the good news is that Schoen has given Harbaugh the tools to try some new things. A quarterback with a big arm and mobility, whether he starts or comes in as a package. A rookie tackle who can protect the blind side if Thomas is limited. A running back who just proved he can block, catch, and score. A defensive tackle who changes the run defense when he is on the field. A safety playing with his hair on fire. And an edge rusher who is winning more than anyone realizes.

Here is what I will be watching against the Texans. Number one, the quarterback. Who starts, and how much of McCarthy do we see? Number two, the running back snaps. Does Tracy get the first crack at being the back Harbaugh is looking for? Number three, the left tackle spot. If Andrew Thomas is limited, can J.C. Davis hold up for a full game? And number four, the run defense. How much does D.J. Reader play, and does anybody else step up next to him?

Joe Schoen has taken his share of criticism over the years, and some of it was fair. Not every move hits. Remember the Darius Slayton contract, $36 million for a receiver the Giants ended up releasing before week one, who then could not get a single offensive snap in three games with the Colts. That one did not work, and you cannot pretend it did. Every general manager in this league has a list of misses.

But here is the difference between a front office that is struggling and one that is building. A struggling front office keeps making the same expensive mistakes. A building front office learns, adjusts, and finds value in the places other teams overlook. Look at what this front office did after Jackson Dart went down. It did not panic and trade a first-round pick for a quarterback. It made a measured move for McCarthy that cost very little. Look at the draft. It did not just chase the big names at the top. It found a tackle on day three who just played like the second-best lineman on the team. That is the kind of process that keeps a team competitive even when injuries pile up, and this team has had more injuries than almost anybody.

And the moves that are paying off right now were not the headline signings. They were a fifth-round trade, a day-three draft pick, and a fifth-round running back. That is how good front offices build depth, and depth is what wins a division like this one. The NFC East is wide open. The Giants are in first place. And with the weapons already in this building, I still believe what I have been saying all season. This team can go all the way.
