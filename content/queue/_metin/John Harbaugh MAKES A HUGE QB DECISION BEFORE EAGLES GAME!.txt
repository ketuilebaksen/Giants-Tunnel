The quarterback decision is made, and it is bigger than one game. Heading into Philadelphia, J.J. McCarthy is the Giants’ starter, and Joe Schoen said this week that McCarthy is their starter right now, at least for this season. Not a one-week audition. Not a package. The guy, for the rest of the year. And John Harbaugh’s decision does not stop at the quarterback. It means building the rest of this season around a young quarterback who has been in the building for about a month. And here is the part almost nobody is talking about. Whether that decision works depends heavily on the 2026 rookie class. The rookie guard protecting McCarthy’s right side. The rookie receiver who just became his new favorite target. The rookie tackle who might have to protect his blind side. And the rookie linebacker who is quietly becoming one of the best defenders in the NFC. So today, I am grading all seven Giants draft picks, seven games into the season, and showing you exactly how each one affects the McCarthy era, starting in Philadelphia. Let’s get into it.

First, let me lay out the quarterback decision clearly, because a lot has happened in a short time. After the Saints game, Harbaugh pulled Jameis Winston and named McCarthy the starter for Houston. McCarthy went 20 of 28 for 252 yards, with no interceptions and no fumbles, and the Giants won 27 to 21 in overtime. During the bye, Schoen said McCarthy is the starter for this season, and that having to decide between McCarthy and Jackson Dart someday would be a good problem. So, that is settled. Winston is the backup, and by every account he has embraced that role. He even ran for a touchdown in Houston.

Now, once you commit to a quarterback for the rest of the season, the question changes. It is no longer who plays quarterback. It is, can the roster around him hold up? And with Brian Burns, Tremaine Edmunds, and Jackson Dart out long-term, and Tyrone Tracy Jr. on injured reserve, the answer depends on young players more than ever. So, let’s grade them. And I want to be fair. I am grading each player by what his job has actually been. A top-ten pick starting every week faces different expectations than a sixth-rounder waiting for his chance. That is not a free pass for anybody. It is about asking the right question. Is the player handling what the coaches put on his plate, and is there enough progress to trust him with more?

Let’s start with the easy one. Arvell Reese. The fifth overall pick out of Ohio State has 56 tackles, one and a half sacks, and an interception through seven games. Pro Football Focus has credited him with six pressures. Those numbers show he contributes in a lot of ways. But what interests me most is how involved he stays. He does not need the play to come straight at him to make an impact. He keeps moving until somebody has finished the job.

Against Houston, there was a play where Reese pressured C.J. Stroud, and then, after the ball was thrown, he turned and chased the completion downfield and made the tackle himself. That is the kind of effort I want from a linebacker. He could have watched once the ball left the quarterback’s hand. Instead, he stayed in the play. And after a week where Dennard Wilson called out the defense for not running to the ball, that play said everything. Speed gives you the chance to get there. Deciding to keep running is what turns speed into something useful.

His coverage has been encouraging too. Through week seven, PFF gave him a coverage grade of 72.8, ninth among 75 qualified linebackers. I expected his athleticism to show up early. I was more interested in how quickly he would handle the responsibilities of playing inside, especially when offenses try to make him hesitate. So far, he is giving us reasons to trust him beyond just chasing down runners. For me, that is an A-plus start, and he belongs in the Defensive Rookie of the Year conversation.

And here is why Reese matters even more now. With Tremaine Edmunds out for the season, the Giants lost the linebacker who called the signals and played every defensive snap. Micah McFadden stepped in brilliantly in Houston, with 10 solo tackles. But Reese is the long-term answer in the middle of this defense, and he is only 21 years old. The next challenge for him is that teams now know what he does well. They are going to try to make him uncomfortable somewhere else. How he adjusts will tell us whether he is a good rookie or a future star. My bet is on the star.

And think about what is coming after Philadelphia. Washington comes to MetLife, with Jayden Daniels, one of the most dangerous running quarterbacks in football. Harbaugh has said the biggest weakness of this defense has been explosive plays on quarterback scrambles. Tyler Shough ran all over the Giants in the Saints game. A linebacker with Reese’s speed and coverage skills is exactly the type of player you can use as a spy, mirroring the quarterback and taking away the scramble lanes. That is a huge responsibility for a 21-year-old rookie. But based on what I have seen, he is ready for it.

Now, the receiver. Malachi Fields. The Giants traded up to take him 74th overall in the third round, so there was a real investment behind this pick. Through seven games, he has 16 catches for 248 yards and a touchdown. And remember, he has already caught passes from three different quarterbacks, Jackson Dart, Jameis Winston, and now McCarthy. That does not excuse everything, but timing and communication have not had the chance to settle every week.

The encouraging part is the recent involvement. Fields had eight targets against New Orleans and five against Houston, 13 over those two games. And his first start with McCarthy produced four catches for 83 yards, including a 47-yard catch and run on an out route. That is the connection I want them to build on after the bye. When a young quarterback sees that a receiver can finish a tough catch or turn a short completion into something bigger, it gives him another option he can trust. Fields has 99 yards after the catch and zero drops in PFF’s tracking. I like that, because his value does not depend only on perfect deep balls. He can make plays with the ball in his hands. And he threw a key block on Devin Singletary’s game-winning touchdown in Houston. Those details matter when you are trying to stay involved in an offense with Malik Nabers and Isaiah Likely.

I am giving him a B-plus, with a real chance to become a dependable second receiver. What I want next is more consistent involvement downfield. He does not have to take over the offense to justify the pick. But if defenses have to respect him outside, that creates another problem for them while they are already trying to stop Nabers and Likely. And for McCarthy, a third reliable target might be the difference between a good offense and a great one.

And Fields has the frame to become that guy. He is a big receiver, around 6 foot 4, who played at Virginia before transferring to Notre Dame. Size like that gives a young quarterback a target he can trust in tight windows, on contested catches, and in the red zone, where the field shrinks and everything gets harder. Nabers wins with speed and route running. Likely wins over the middle. Fields can win with size on the outside. Three different types of weapons for three different situations. That is how you build a passing game that defenses cannot take away with one plan.

Now, a tougher evaluation. Francis Mauigoa. The tenth overall pick, the pick that came to the Giants in the Dexter Lawrence trade, moved from tackle in college to right guard in the NFL. And you can see why that transition takes time. Inside, there is very little room to recover once your balance or hand placement goes wrong. Through week seven, PFF charged him with 13 pressures allowed, one sack, four quarterback hits, and eight hurries. His overall grade of 49.1 ranked 61st among 74 qualified guards. That is below what I want from a top-ten pick, even with a position change.

But Houston gave us a reason to look past the rough overall number. Mauigoa did not allow a single pressure in that game. He helped create the lane for Cam Skattebo’s 42-yard run in overtime. And he made another important block on Singletary’s 30-yard game-winning touchdown. Those plays show exactly what the Giants were looking for when they drafted him. He can move people. And the encouraging part was not just the strength. It was that he got himself in the right position, so he could use that strength without losing control of the block.

I am giving Mauigoa a C so far. The Houston game helps, but one good afternoon does not erase the earlier problems. And this is where he connects directly to the quarterback decision. If McCarthy is the starter for the rest of the season, the Giants need a clean pocket on the right side. Fewer snaps where a defender wins before the play has a chance to develop. If Mauigoa can make his pass protection more dependable while keeping that power in the run game, the entire right side of the line benefits, and so does the young quarterback behind it. He is 21 and learning, but the standard is clear. Give your quarterback room to operate.

And Harbaugh has seen this exact situation before. In 2016, the Ravens drafted Ronnie Stanley sixth overall, and his rookie season had real ups and downs. People questioned the pick. A few years later, he was a first-team All-Pro left tackle. Offensive linemen often take longer to develop than players at other positions, because the technique is so precise and the jump in strength and speed from college to the NFL is so big. I am not saying Mauigoa will become Ronnie Stanley. I am saying a rough first half of a rookie season does not define an offensive lineman’s career. What I want to see is the trend, and Houston was a step in the right direction.

Next, Colton Hood, and this is the one that worries me. The Giants selected him 37th overall in the second round, and he has appeared in six games. But that does not mean six games of real defensive work. He has not played a defensive snap since week three. He missed the Saints game with a concussion, and he returned against Houston only on special teams. So, yes, injury has interrupted his start. But the bigger question is why he has not earned his place back in the defensive rotation, especially with so many injuries in the secondary. His early coverage results were a big part of the concern. And with Paulson Adebo now back from injured reserve, Deonte Banks playing the best football of his career, and Joey Porter Jr. on the other side, Hood’s path to snaps is not getting easier. For now, I am calling it incomplete with a concern. He is young, and second-round picks deserve patience. But the bye week is a chance for him to show the coaches he is ready, because this defense needs every healthy corner it can get.

Now, a player who made a positive impression with far less attention. J.C. Davis. The Giants took him 192nd overall in the sixth round, and he stepped in at left tackle against the Saints when Andrew Thomas was being evaluated for a concussion. Davis played 25 offensive snaps without allowing a single pressure. Now, those were not all pass-blocking snaps, so let’s keep the sample in perspective. But coming off the bench and protecting the quarterback without immediately becoming the weak point is a real contribution from a sixth-round rookie. I give that limited work a B-minus.

And Davis connects to the quarterback decision too. A backup tackle does not get to choose when his chance comes or how much time he gets to prepare. He just has to be ready. With Andrew Thomas dealing with injuries all season and Jermaine Eluemunor in a walking boot with a calf injury after Houston, there is a real chance Davis is protecting McCarthy at some point in the next few weeks. Knowing he held up against New Orleans should give the coaches some confidence. I could see him developing some flexibility at guard eventually, but that is my thought, not a team plan. For now, being a reliable backup tackle is enough.

Then there is Bobby Jamison-Travis, the Auburn defensive lineman the Giants took 186th overall. He waited through the early part of the season before playing 18 snaps against New Orleans and 24 against Houston. And the second appearance was more encouraging, especially against the run. At 25, he is older than most rookies, but age does not automatically give him polished NFL technique. I want him holding his ground, staying in his gap, and making it harder for blockers to clear space through the middle. His grade is incomplete. But the Giants need defensive line depth, especially with Dexter Lawrence gone and D.J. Reader carrying so much of the run defense. Jamison-Travis does not have to become a pass rusher to help. If he can handle run downs, the staff has more freedom with everyone else. And with veteran Dean Lowry joining the practice squad, the competition for those snaps just got more interesting.

And finally, Jack Kelly, the BYU linebacker taken 193rd overall. He has not played a defensive snap, but he has earned a regular special teams role. Against Washington, he nearly blocked a punt. That is how a late-round pick gives the staff a reason to dress him every week. His college pass-rushing background makes him interesting, especially with Burns and Edmunds out. But those injuries do not automatically create a role for him. Dennard Wilson still has to decide which responsibilities fit him and whether he is ready. For now, he is a special teams contributor with upside.

And step back for a second and look at the bigger picture of how this roster has been built through the draft. The 2024 class gave the Giants Malik Nabers and Tyrone Tracy Jr. The 2025 class gave them Abdul Carter, Jackson Dart, and Cam Skattebo. And now the 2026 class has given them Arvell Reese, Francis Mauigoa, Malachi Fields, and a sixth-round tackle who already held up in a real game. That is a young core spread across every level of the roster. Not every pick has hit. Colton Hood still has to prove himself, and Mauigoa has work to do. But when you look at the three classes together, you see a franchise that is building something that could last well beyond this season.

So, let’s put it all together. One A-plus. One B-plus. One B-minus. One C. And three incompletes, one of them with a real concern. For a rookie class seven games in, that is a solid start, especially with the top pick looking like a future star. And Giants fans know how much a rookie class can matter in a playoff run. Back in 2007, the Giants’ rookie class was a big part of the Super Bowl team. Aaron Ross started at corner. Steve Smith made big catches during the playoff run. Kevin Boss took over at tight end. Ahmad Bradshaw became a weapon at running back. Zak DeOssie became a fixture on special teams. Rookies do not have to be stars to help win a title. They have to be ready when their moment comes.

And that brings it all back to the quarterback decision. Harbaugh is betting the rest of this season on McCarthy. For that bet to pay off, Mauigoa has to protect him. Davis has to be ready if a tackle goes down. Fields has to keep becoming a reliable target. And on defense, Reese has to keep making plays all over the field, so that McCarthy is not stuck playing from behind. That is how a young quarterback and a young roster grow together.

Harbaugh has talked about where development can happen without weakening the game plan on Sunday. That is the balance with this rookie class. You want Reese and Fields getting more responsibility. You want Mauigoa learning through the hard snaps. You want Hood fighting his way back into the rotation. But you cannot sacrifice wins to do it, not when you are 4 and 2 and 1 and in first place.

Here is what I will be watching in Philadelphia. Number one, how McCarthy handles a hostile division road game, his second start as a Giant. Number two, Mauigoa against the Eagles’ interior pass rush, because that is a real test for a rookie guard. Number three, how many targets Fields gets, and whether the McCarthy connection keeps growing. And number four, Reese, because I want to see how he handles an offense that has now studied him for seven weeks.

The quarterback decision is made. Now it is up to the young players around him to make it pay off. And after what this rookie class showed in Houston, I like their chances. This team can go all the way.
