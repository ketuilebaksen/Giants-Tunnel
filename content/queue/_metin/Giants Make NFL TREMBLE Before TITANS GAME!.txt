Let's start with just how loud the hype got, because it was everywhere. On national television, an analyst looked straight into the camera and said it. The New York Giants will win the NFC East. Not compete for it. Win it. And the reasoning was not crazy. He did not like what he saw from the Dallas defense, and we watched that up close, did we not? He was not impressed with the Commanders. And the Eagles, in his words, looked ready to be beat. Then came the part that made every Giants fan smile. What John Harbaugh is doing with this team is real. They are playing real football. My guy Jackson Dart is going to ball. And Skattebo? Who is going to tackle him? He is a little rhino. You are not tackling a rhino. Folks, when was the last time you heard national voices talk about the New York Giants like that in September? The bandwagon got so full that people were joking about a Giants and Jets Super Bowl. Was it getting a little crazy? Absolutely. But it tells you how completely one night against Dallas changed the perception of this franchise.

And the thing is, the hype was earned. It was not built on one lucky bounce. Every Giants fan who watched that Dallas game saw the same things the experts saw. A clear game plan. A better organized team. A team that controlled the ball exactly when it needed to control the ball and was simply the more physical side all night. The decisive way Dart played, first read taken away, boom, on to number two, no hesitation. He looked different. And here is the stat that explains why the country suddenly believed. Last season, this team held double digit leads in the fourth quarter six times and lost every single one of those games. Six blown leads. That was the identity, the held breath, the when is it going to fall apart feeling every fan knows too well. Against Dallas, they finished. And the credit went straight to Harbaugh, because he has taught this team accountability, confidence, and one simple idea. You play to win. You do not play not to lose. That is a culture change you could see through the television screen.

But while the fans were booking Super Bowl tickets, the sportsbooks were sending a very different message. The Giants opened as seven point underdogs in Los Angeles. Seven. Coming off that statement win, flying as high as this franchise has flown in years, and the market still said not yet. The sharpest guys on television were all landing in the same place. Rams win, Giants cover. One of them called it 28 to 24 Los Angeles. And the reasoning behind those picks, that is where the real story lives, because they laid out three warnings, and every single one of them deserves our attention now.

Warning number one, the Rams running game. This got swept under the rug after their sleepy opener, but if you actually watched their week one tape against San Francisco, the Rams ran the football well. There were moments where their backs hit the second and third level untouched, big holes, linebackers turning around, safeties making desperation dives. The concern was simple. If New York cannot slow that run game down, everything else collapses. Stafford gets easy situations. Play action becomes lethal. The whole defense gets stretched. And here is the deeper problem. New York wants to control games with its own run game. A team that runs it right back at you takes that control away. Control the line of scrimmage or lose the night. That was the warning, folks.

And notice something about that first warning, because this is where the front office deserves a standing ovation. What was the biggest weakness the tape showed? Stopping the run. What did the Giants just trade for? Daron Payne, one of the most punishing interior run defenders in football, now planted next to Dexter Lawrence in the middle of this defense. The national guys spent all week worrying about backs reaching the second and third level against New York. The front office watched the same tape and went out and bought a wall. That is not a coincidence, folks. That is an organization that sees its own flaws clearly and attacks them immediately, and it is exactly why that trade matters so much more than a headline. The single question that decides whether this defense is exciting or legitimate, can you stop the run, just got a very large, very angry new answer.

Warning number two, Matthew Stafford. And I found this conversation fascinating, because the analysts threaded a needle that I think is exactly right. On one hand, expecting Stafford to play as poorly as he did in the opener two weeks in a row was always a fantasy. He was coming off that whole Australia mess, and even Sean McVay admitted the travel plan wrecked his team. But on the other hand, when people call Stafford the best quarterback in football, the honest answer is, no, stop. Not at this stage of his career. Here is the difference. The truly elite guys right now, your Josh Allen, your Lamar, your Burrow, they survive chaos. Blown protection? No problem, they move, they escape, they turn broken plays into first downs. Stafford is not that anymore. He needs systematic help. He needs protection, he needs structure, he needs the run game doing the heavy lifting. But, and this is the scary part, give the man a clean pocket and he will still carve up anybody in the sport. So the entire question was always going to be the same one. Does Stafford spend the night comfortable, or does he spend it running for his life? That is not a talent question. That is a trenches question.

And connect that Stafford profile to our side of the ball for a second, because it explains why the pass rush is the heartbeat of everything this defense wants to be. A quarterback who needs a clean pocket to be great is a quarterback you beat by never giving him one. That is why the Brian Burns news mattering so much this week is not just an injury update, it is a season update. A healthy Burns bending one edge, Abdul Carter exploding off the other, Lawrence and now Payne caving in the middle, that is the four man rush that lets you play honest coverage behind it and take away the easy answers. Every quality opponent left on this schedule gets easier or harder based on one thing, whether this front gets home without help. The Rams tape was the reminder. When the rush arrives, this defense travels anywhere. When it does not, quarterbacks of Stafford's caliber will make you pay full price.

Warning number three, and this one came with a number attached. If the Rams came out in heavy personnel, twelve and thirteen personnel, extra tight ends, max protection to keep Stafford upright, then Puka Nacua could have a two hundred yard game. That was the actual phrase used on air. Two hundred yards. Legitimately. Because when you force a defense to honor the run first, the play action windows behind it get enormous, and Nacua is the best in football at living in those windows. Now, the counter argument was that you can double Nacua all night and live comfortably with Davante Adams one on one, because at this point of his career, Adams is not scaring anybody between the twenties. The one place you still game plan for him is the red zone, where his route running remains elite. But add it all up and the picture was clear. The Rams had a mathematical answer for everything. Protect Stafford with bodies, run the ball to set the table, and let Nacua feast on a defense stretched too thin.

Now, here is where I want to zoom out, because tucked inside all this Giants talk was a segment about the Chiefs that might be the most relevant thing anybody said all week. Kansas City just gave a masterclass in something we suddenly care about very much, protecting a quarterback who cannot carry the load. Mahomes is working his way back from that knee, so what did Andy Reid do? He handed the football to Kenneth Walker, the man they brought over from Seattle, and Walker went off for 173 yards on the ground. Sixty percent of the time on first down, Reid called a run. Sixty percent. And what happens when you commit to the run like that? You eat the clock. You stop depending on your quarterback's arm. Your defense stays fresh, and Kansas City's defense responded with four sacks and two turnovers. Run it, run it, run it, play great defense, take a deep shot every now and then, win the football game. It is the oldest formula in the book. And folks, look me in the eye. That is not just the Chiefs story. That is the exact blueprint for the New York Giants right now, with our quarterback situation what it is. If Andy Reid can build a game plan that protects Patrick Mahomes, then this staff can absolutely build one that protects whoever lines up under center for us.

Because here is the beautiful thing about the formula the experts laid out for this team. It never depended on the name on the back of the quarterback's jersey. Nobody was asking Jackson Dart to throw for 400 yards. The ask was smart decisions, staying aggressive, keeping the offense on schedule.
