John Harbaugh just said the quiet part out loud. Standing in front of reporters after the overtime win in Houston, the Giants head coach did not talk about a nice road win or a good week of practice. He said the next two games, Philadelphia and then a quick turnaround after that, will be the two most important games of the season. He said those two games will set the direction for the rest of the year. And then he said this. We’re going to be in this thing to the end now. Folks, think about who is saying that. This is a coach who won a Super Bowl. A coach who does not hand out hype for free. And he is telling his locker room, and every Giants fan, that this team is no longer playing for respect. It is playing for the division. Today, I am going to break down exactly what Harbaugh said, why the national media suddenly cannot stop talking about the Giants, what Joe Schoen just told us about the trade deadline, and why the next ten days might be the most important stretch this franchise has had in years. Let’s get into it.

Let me start with the exact words, because the words matter here. Harbaugh called the win in Houston a massive energy shot in the arm. He called it a reward. But then he immediately added the warning. You have to make something of it. It does not mean anything if you do not build on it. It does not mean the Giants are going to win a championship yet. It means they have an opportunity. And then he laid out the plan. The coaching staff is thinking about two games over the bye week, not one. Philadelphia, and then a quick turnaround right after that. Two most important games of the season, in his words. Games that will set the direction for the rest of the season.

Now, why is that statement such a big deal? Because head coaches almost never do this. The standard coaching script is one game at a time. Every coach in America says it. The next game is the most important game, and we are not looking ahead. Harbaugh broke that script on purpose. He told the world that his staff is preparing for a two-game stretch as one unit. That tells you how much he believes is on the line. And it tells you he wants his players to feel that pressure now, during the bye, instead of discovering it on November 8th in Philadelphia.

And there is another line from that press conference that I keep coming back to. Harbaugh said this was a pivotal game, a really high pressure game for the team. He said the coaches did not even talk about that with the players, but they all knew how important it was, and they responded accordingly. That is a coach telling you something about his locker room. He did not have to manufacture urgency. The players already felt it. And when a team feels the stakes on its own and still plays clean, physical football on the road, that is a team that is starting to grow up.

You could hear it in the locker room after the game too. The message from the postgame speech was simple. This is just the beginning. This group is so talented, but the secret sauce is resilience, the will to finish no matter what it looks like. Think about how this team got here. A season-ending injury to the starting quarterback on his seventh snap of week two. A blowout loss to the Rams. A tie in Washington that felt like a loss. A painful loss to the Saints. Three different quarterbacks taking meaningful snaps. And yet here they are, 4 and 2 and 1, in first place in the NFC East, unbeaten in the division. If that is not resilience, I do not know what is.

Now, let’s talk about the national reaction, because it has been loud. For weeks, the Giants were an afterthought on national television. Now, suddenly, they are a main topic. One analyst said it plainly. The Giants have an identity as a football team, and nobody else in the division does. He described them as a physical offensive line, talented playmakers, a team that can run the football at you and wants to throw it downfield. On defense, an active defensive line, an athletic front seven, and a secondary that tries to lock you up. And then he said he cannot say that about Philadelphia, he cannot say that about Dallas, and he cannot say that about Washington.

I want to sit with that for a second, because I think it is the most important thing anyone has said about this team all year. Identity is not a buzzword. It is the thing that keeps you standing in November and December, when injuries pile up and the weather turns. A team with an identity knows what it is going to do on third and two. It knows who it is going to lean on when the game gets tight. And right now, the Giants know. They are going to run the football, protect it, and hit you. That is who they are.

And then there was the J.J. McCarthy debate, which I loved, because both sides had a point. One side said McCarthy played better than anyone expected. Five completions of 20 or more yards. Two bombs early, 47 and 42 yards in the first quarter. 20 of 28, 252 yards, about 71 percent completions, a 99.1 passer rating, sacked three times, and zero turnovers. Accurate, decisive, poised, and never panicked in the pocket.

The other side pushed back. Not a spectacular performance, they said. A nice, managed game. He made enough throws to keep the defense honest, so Houston could not just stack the box against the run. And the biggest thing he did was protect the football. That same voice argued that this is exactly why Harbaugh went with McCarthy over Jameis Winston. Jameis can make throws, and he can put up 30 points, but he can also get reckless. Harbaugh does not need his quarterback to go win games by himself. He needs him to protect the ball, make throws when the offense needs them, and let the run game do the heavy lifting.

So, who is right? I think they both are. And I think that is the best news of all. Because if McCarthy can be a quarterback who does not turn the ball over and still hits a couple of shots downfield every game, that is the perfect fit for this offense. You do not need a superhero. You need a point guard who can occasionally pull up from deep. And remember the receipt I owe you. Before Houston, I predicted McCarthy would throw two touchdown passes. He threw zero. I was wrong on the stat line, and I already owned it. But I will take zero touchdowns and zero turnovers in a win over two touchdowns and two interceptions in a loss every single day of the week.

And here is where the run game comes in. The Giants ran for 188 yards on 34 carries in Houston, and they scored four rushing touchdowns from four different players, Tyrone Tracy Jr., Cam Skattebo, Devin Singletary, and Jameis Winston. That was the first time the Giants did that since December 3rd, 1950. When this team runs the ball, it looks like one team. When you stop the run, it looks like a totally different team. That is the honest truth about the Giants right now, and it is why everything Harbaugh does is built around that ground game.

And one of the most interesting points in that national debate was about Skattebo. The argument was that Harbaugh’s decision to keep him on the field but not give him a single carry against the Saints, after the overtime fumble in Washington, went a long way. What showed up in Houston was a football player. Not someone chasing attention or celebrity, but a back reminding everyone why he is on this roster. 17 carries, 124 yards, a touchdown, and the 42-yard run in overtime that set up the winning score. Whether you agree with how that was described or not, you cannot argue with the result. Harbaugh drew a line, and his running back answered it.

Now, let’s talk about the defense, because this part shocked people too. The Giants lost Tremaine Edmunds for the season. They lost Brian Burns to a torn ACL. And Dexter Lawrence is no longer here, after being traded in the offseason. Three of the most important names on that defense, gone. And yet against Houston, the Giants held C.J. Stroud to 153 passing yards and the Texans to just 84 rushing yards. That is 237 total yards, against a Texans offense with real talent. The Giants out-gained Houston 420 to 237.

How did they do it? Part of it was the run game, because when your offense controls the ball, the other team’s offense has fewer chances. Part of it was rookie linebacker Arvell Reese, who keeps getting more responsibility. Part of it was Micah McFadden, who had 10 solo tackles. And part of it was a defensive line that kept collapsing the pocket even without its biggest names. The defense does not have the same star power it had in August. But it has discipline, effort, and a plan. And that is enough when your offense is running the football like it did in Houston.

And while the Giants were celebrating, Houston was facing hard questions. One of the loudest voices on national sports television went right at DeMeco Ryans. The point was simple. Last year, the Texans started 0 and 3 before going on a run in the second half of the season. This year, they started 0 and 4. So what are they doing in the offseason, in training camp, and in the preseason to get the team ready for September? I am not here to pile on Houston. But I mention it because it shows you how the Giants are being viewed now. The Giants are no longer the story about a team in trouble. They are the team causing trouble for other people.

Now, let’s get to the part every Giants fan is waiting for. The trade deadline. It is on November 10th. And look at the calendar. The Giants play in Philadelphia on November 8th. Then the deadline hits two days later. Then they play Washington on a short week. That means Joe Schoen will make his final deadline decisions sandwiched right in between Harbaugh’s two most important games of the season. You cannot script a more dramatic window than that.

And Schoen gave us real insight into how he is thinking. He said there are a lot of factors. Where the Giants are pick-wise is one of them. Right now, he said, the Giants do not have a fourth or a fifth-round pick. In some situations, that may rule some players out. Maybe a player’s value is a third, and a third is too rich. Maybe a sixth is not enough. So then the Giants start looking at future picks, contract status, and where they are on the depth chart. He also said something I really respect. It is easy to say go sign X, Y, and Z. But if a team is not really ready to get rid of a player, or it is asking too much, and that does not match the value the Giants see, then it does not make sense.

Let me translate that. Schoen is telling you he is willing to be aggressive, but he is not going to be reckless. And that is the right approach. The missing fifth-round pick went to Minnesota in the J.J. McCarthy trade, and that trade looks pretty smart right now. The missing fourth is part of why the Giants have to be careful. They do have their first, second, and third-round picks in 2027, plus two sixths, and a projected compensatory fourth. But a compensatory pick is not guaranteed until the league awards it. So every trade conversation is going to be about finding the right price, not just the right player.

So, what position makes the most sense? A lot of people are pointing at running back, and I understand why. Tyrone Tracy Jr. is on injured reserve with a hamstring. He has to miss at least four games, which means Philadelphia, Washington, Jacksonville, and Indianapolis. Cam Skattebo felt his own hamstring grab on that 42-yard run in overtime. Devin Singletary is the backup. An undrafted rookie off the Chargers’ practice squad is the third back. When your entire identity is the run game, that is a thin room.

One analyst floated an interesting idea. Tony Pollard. And I want to be clear, this is a panel idea, not a report. Nobody has said the Giants are talking about Pollard. But the logic is interesting. Add Pollard to a room with Skattebo, Singletary, and eventually a returning Tracy, and the room just becomes flat-out better. Pollard has had real success in his career, and a lot of it came in the NFC East with Dallas. He knows these defenses. He knows these stadiums. And his price tag would likely be a lot more reasonable than the other big name everyone keeps bringing up.

That other name is Jonathan Taylor. We have talked about the Colts connection on this channel before, and Jordan Raanan has hinted at it in the past. The Colts have already been sellers, trading Quenton Nelson to Seattle and DeForest Buckner to San Francisco. But the analyst on that panel made a smart point. The price on Taylor is going to be as good as the player is. He is a tremendous back, and the Colts may simply sit back and say, we want a first-round pick, and if we do not get it, we are not trading him. For a team with no fourth and no fifth this year, that is a very expensive conversation.

So here is where I land, and this is my opinion, not a report. I would be stunned if the Giants gave up a first-round pick for a running back in the middle of a season. Not because Taylor is not worth it on the field. But because running back is a position where you can find production without paying a premium, and the Giants have already proven that with Skattebo, a fourth-round pick, and Tracy, a fifth-round pick. If Schoen adds a back, I think it is more likely to be a value move, a veteran who can carry 10 to 12 times a game until Tracy returns. A Pollard-type move, not necessarily Pollard himself.

And I really liked how that same analyst framed the deadline. If I am the Giants, he said, the question is how can I improve myself, win this division, give myself a chance to go on a run, but also position myself for next year. This is not an all-or-nothing year. And I completely agree. This team has a rookie class that is starting to look special, a young quarterback situation that is still being figured out, and a head coach in his first year. You do not mortgage that future for one push. But you also do not waste a season where you are in first place in November. That is the balance Schoen has to strike.

And remember what Schoen said during the bye. J.J. McCarthy is the starter, at least for this season. The team will look at every deadline option. And Ryan Dunleavy, who covers the Giants closely, expects the Giants to be aggressive. So put it all together. The Giants are going to be active. They are going to make calls. But they are going to make moves that fit the value they see, not the value the headlines want.

Now, let’s get to the real test. Philadelphia. The Eagles are without Jalen Hurts. They have been stuck in a losing streak, and they just lost 38 to 21 to Dallas on Monday night. On paper, it looks like the perfect time to go into Philadelphia. But I want to be careful here, because that is exactly the kind of thinking that gets a team beaten. A desperate team, at home, in a division game, with its season slipping away, is one of the most dangerous opponents in football. The Eagles will have their crowd, their pride, and nothing to lose.

And think about what a win would mean. The Giants have not won the NFC East since 2011. They are unbeaten in the division right now, with a win over Dallas and a tie in Washington. A win in Philadelphia would put the Eagles in a deep hole and give the Giants a road division win over the team that has owned this division for years. Then they come home for Washington, a team they already tied, on a short week. Win both, and the Giants are not just leading the division. They are in control of it.

But lose both? That is the other side of Harbaugh’s statement, and I think it is why he said it. If the Giants come out of the bye flat and drop two straight division games, the whole picture changes. The tie in Washington would feel a lot worse. The confidence from Houston would disappear. And the trade deadline decisions in the middle of that stretch would look completely different. That is what he meant when he said these two games will set the direction for the rest of the season. It is not hype. It is math.

And here is a quiet stat that matters a lot. The Giants have scored 163 points and allowed 161. That is a plus-2 point differential through seven games. It tells you this team has been in a lot of close games. It also tells you there is not much margin for error. The Giants are not blowing people out. They are winning with toughness, the run game, and timely plays. That works, but only if you protect the football and avoid the big mistakes. That is why Harbaugh’s message about ball security and McCarthy’s zero-turnover game matter so much.

And there is one more thing that worries me heading into those two games. The kicking. Dominic Zvada missed two first-half field goals in Houston, and the coaches never sent him back out after halftime. He made his first nine field goal attempts of the season, but he has gone just 4 of 9 since. In close division games, a missed kick can be the difference between a win and a tie or a loss. The Giants already know what a tie feels like. I would not be surprised if the coaching staff brings in a kicker for a workout over the bye, just to create competition. That is speculation on my part. But it is something I am watching.

So, let me give you my honest take on Harbaugh’s statement. I love it. I love that he is not hiding from the moment. I love that he is putting the pressure on now, while the team has time to absorb it. And I love that he is pairing that pressure with perspective. A win in Houston does not mean a championship. It means an opportunity. That is the right message for a young team that is learning how to win.
