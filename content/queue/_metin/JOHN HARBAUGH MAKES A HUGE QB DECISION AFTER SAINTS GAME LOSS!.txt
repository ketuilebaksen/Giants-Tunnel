John Harbaugh has made his quarterback call after the Saints loss, and it is not the clean, simple answer either side of this fanbase wanted. Jameis Winston is not being benched. But J.J. McCarthy is no longer just an emergency button for desperation time. His package is getting bigger, his role in the game plan is growing, and heading into Houston, the Giants are officially moving toward a two-quarterback plan. Folks, that is a huge decision, because it changes how this offense operates every single week. And it comes with real risk. Because a week ago, the loudest criticism of this exact idea was simple. Why would you take snaps away from a quarterback who just threw for 326 yards and three touchdowns? Today, I am going to give you both sides of that argument, the numbers behind it, and two other stories that tell you exactly how Harbaugh makes decisions, including a former Giants receiver whose story just took a stunning turn. Let’s get into it.

If you are new here, hit that subscribe button, it costs you nothing and it helps this channel more than you know. And before we start, drop one word in the comments. Winston, McCarthy, or both. I want to see where this fanbase stands right now.

Let me be clear about what I am calling the decision here, because I always tell you the difference between what is confirmed and what is my read. Right after the Saints game, Harbaugh said the quarterback question was not up for discussion ten minutes after a game, and that the evaluation process starts right now. He also said he has no problem with Jameis whatsoever. And even before the Saints game, Harbaugh had already talked about expanding McCarthy’s package. Put those two things together, and here is how I see Harbaugh’s direction. Winston remains the starter for now. McCarthy gets more. More snaps, more concepts, more responsibility. That is the plan I expect the Giants to take to Houston. And if the evaluation tips further after this week, that line between starter and package guy could get very thin, very quickly.

Now, think about how we got here. The Giants lost to the Saints at MetLife. A fumbled kickoff return at the end of the first half let New Orleans back in, the defense could not get a stop out of the locker room, and the Giants fell behind big. Harbaugh turned to McCarthy in what he called desperation time. McCarthy made plays, got the Giants back into it, and had them throwing the ball into the end zone at the end with a two-point try waiting. Harbaugh said McCarthy did a heck of a job, and that you have got to compliment him. And when asked about McCarthy’s mobility, he called it a big asset, a big part of his skill set. So, the decision did not come out of nowhere. It came out of tape.

But I want to be fair, so let me give you the strongest case against this decision, because it was being made loudly even before Sunday. Remember how McCarthy got here. Jackson Dart suffered a season-ending knee injury, and the Giants traded with the Minnesota Vikings last month to get McCarthy, reportedly giving up a fifth-round draft pick. That part made sense to everybody. A roster needs depth, and one injury can change everything overnight. But depth is one thing. Designing special plays to bring the backup onto the field while the starter is producing is a very different conversation.

And look at what happened in Washington. McCarthy played four snaps. On one of them, he completed a screen pass to Malik Nabers that would have moved the chains, but the play was wiped out by an illegal shift penalty. Other than that, he produced zero yards. Now, four snaps are not enough to evaluate a quarterback’s ceiling. But they were enough to show that the package had not yet given New York a measurable advantage. So, when Harbaugh talked about expanding it, critics asked the obvious question. Expanding what, exactly?

And there is a deeper warning in that argument that I think every Giants fan should hear. The coaching question should always come first. Which quarterback, and which concept, give the team its best chance on this play? If the answer is McCarthy in certain situations, the package makes sense. But if the answer is simply that the team spent a draft pick and wants to justify it, that is a dangerous reason to disrupt an offense. Teams fall into that trap all the time. They pay for something, and then they feel pressure to use it, even when the tape says otherwise.

Now, here is why I think Harbaugh’s decision is not about the fifth-round pick, and why the Saints game changed the conversation. Before Sunday, the McCarthy package was a theory. Four snaps, one screen pass, one penalty. After Sunday, there is real tape of McCarthy running an offense in a high-pressure, two-minute style situation, moving the football, and giving the team a chance to win at the end. Is that the same as starting a game? No. Harbaugh himself said desperation time is different than normal football. Defenses play softer when they have a big lead. But it is more evidence than the Giants had a week ago, and evidence is what this decision should be built on.

And then there is the football argument that matters most to me. The running game. Harbaugh did not sugarcoat it after the Saints game. Not very good. Not even close to good enough. The Giants have struggled to run efficiently all season, especially on first down, and against New Orleans, Harbaugh rotated four running backs, Cam Skattebo, Breece Hall, Najee Harris, and another back he wanted to get into the mix, because nobody has grabbed the job. That is a problem Matt Nagy has to solve, and a mobile quarterback is one of the few tools that can change the math for a struggling run game.

Here is how it works. When a defense has to account for the quarterback keeping the football, the backside defender cannot just crash down on the handoff. He has to hesitate. He has to stay home in case the quarterback pulls it and runs. That hesitation, even half a second, can open a crease for the running back. A designed keeper, a read-option look, or motion paired with a quarterback run can create problems that simply do not exist when the quarterback is a pocket passer. Now, I want to be careful. These are examples of what a mobile quarterback package can do. Harbaugh has not promised any specific plays. But think about McCarthy and Breece Hall in a read-option look, with Hall’s speed to the edge and McCarthy’s legs on the keep. That is a different kind of stress than anything Jameis Winston can put on a defense.

But I also want to be honest about the limits. A gadget can steal an advantage. It cannot become the entire offensive identity. If the Giants start turning every possession into a coaching experiment, they risk breaking the rhythm of the quarterback who has actually been carrying this offense.

And let’s talk about that quarterback, because Jameis Winston has earned a lot of respect on this channel. He threw for 326 yards and three touchdowns in Washington, leading a comeback from 14 points down in the fourth quarter. He went unbeaten in his first three starts, two wins and a tie. He became the first Giant since Eli Manning in 2016 to throw three touchdowns in back-to-back games. Winston has always been an aggressive quarterback, and that aggression produces both spectacular throws and costly mistakes. Against the Saints, Harbaugh called his day up and down. But when he is on, he moves this offense, and that is not something a coaching staff should casually interrupt.

That is the tension in this decision. Harbaugh is trying to add a new wrinkle without slowing down a quarterback who can get hot. And quarterbacks are rhythm players. When a starter comes off the field for a series of package plays, he can lose his feel for the game, especially in a no-huddle, tempo offense that depends on momentum. So, the challenge for Harbaugh and Nagy is not just designing McCarthy plays. It is deciding when to use them. On early downs when the run game needs help? In the red zone, where mobility can buy extra time? Or only in specific situations where the defense is most vulnerable to a quarterback run? The timing matters as much as the plays.

And there is a perfect example of this kind of plan working in recent NFL history, and the man in the booth for the Giants’ broadcasts this season knows it better than anybody. Drew Brees. In New Orleans, Sean Payton built packages for Taysom Hill while Brees was the starter. Hill would come in on certain downs as a runner, a blocker, a receiver, or a quarterback, and defenses had to prepare for him every single week. But Brees stayed the starter, and the offense still ran through him. It worked because the package was a complement, not a competition. Then, when Brees got hurt in 2020, Hill stepped in as the starter and went 3 and 1. That is the ideal version of what Harbaugh is building. A package that makes the starter’s offense harder to defend, and a backup who is ready if the job ever comes to him. The danger is when a package becomes a weekly referendum on the starter. Then, nobody is comfortable.

Now, the second story connects directly to the quarterback decision, because whoever is throwing the ball needs somebody to throw it to beyond the two stars. Look at how concentrated the Giants passing game has become. Against Washington, Malik Nabers and Isaiah Likely combined for 63.5 percent of Jameis Winston’s targets. Nearly two of every three throws went to those two players. And they have earned it. Likely ranks second among tight ends in target share, and across weeks four and five, Nabers ranked fourth among receivers. They are the center of this offense for a reason.

But here is the problem. A plan that works against one vulnerable secondary can become a trap when better coverage units show up. Opponents can tilt safety help toward Nabers, put more attention on Likely, and dare somebody else to beat them. And look at the calendar. Two of the Giants’ next three opponents are Houston and Philadelphia, teams known for strong secondaries. They will not necessarily give Nabers and Likely the freedom Washington did. That is when a third pass catcher stops being a luxury and becomes a necessity.

And that brings us to rookie receiver Malachi Fields. You know the training camp story. A newcomer turns heads at practice, makes highlight catches in preseason, and fans start imagining a breakout year. Then the real games begin, and the offense keeps looking elsewhere. Through the first five games, Fields had exactly two catches in each game. That is ten receptions, and no obvious expansion of his role. And here is the thing. The door has opened for him. The Giants brought in six wide receivers in the offseason. JuJu Smith-Schuster and Odell Beckham Jr. were released. Calvin Austin III and Braxton Berrios suffered season-ending injuries. That leaves Darnell Mooney and Fields among the newcomers who are still standing. This is not a rookie blocked by a parade of veterans. This is a rookie with a real path to more targets.

So, how do you get him more involved without forcing throws that are not there? Imagine Fields on more quick crossers, simple in-breaking routes, and concepts where the quarterback can get the ball out before the pass rush arrives. Those are possibilities, not confirmed new plays. But think about how that connects to everything else. With the offensive line banged up, quick, easy throws protect the quarterback. Against Houston’s secondary, a third option punishes a defense that sells out on Nabers and Likely. And whether it is Winston or McCarthy under center, a young receiver who can win quickly makes life easier for both. Mooney belongs in this conversation too. And do not forget Charlie Jones, who made a great deep catch against the Saints that Harbaugh called awesome. Somebody outside Nabers and Likely has to become a consistent threat, or elite secondaries are going to take this offense away. Does Fields need more opportunities, or does he need to do more to earn them? Honestly, both can be true. Coaches should reward dependable execution, but they also have to create ways for young players to contribute.

Now, the third story, and this one tells you a lot about how John Harbaugh makes decisions. Darius Slayton has been released by the Indianapolis Colts, only weeks after joining them. And for Giants fans, the speed of that move is striking. Remember what Slayton meant to this team. He led the Giants in receiving yards in both 2022 and 2023. Those were not powerhouse offenses, and he was often the guy asked to create a downfield answer when very little else was working. The Giants eventually signed him to a $36 million contract, betting on his experience, familiarity, and vertical speed.

But the offense moved past him. His targets, catches, and yards declined after those seasons. Even when Malik Nabers missed most of 2025, Slayton did not reestablish himself as the centerpiece. Wan’Dale Robinson and Theo Johnson carried more of the load instead. Then came a crowded offseason. Slayton slid down the depth chart in the preseason, survived the cut to 53, and was released before the opener. At the time, Harbaugh praised him and said he would land somewhere and play. Indianapolis became that landing spot. But playing was another matter. Across three games with the Colts, Slayton recorded zero offensive snaps. Zero. He could not get on the field even with the Colts starting Laquon Treadwell and 34-year-old Keenan Allen at receiver. Once the Colts got healthier at the position, they let him go.

Now, I want to be careful with the word mistake. Slayton gave this franchise real seasons. Fans remember the catches, the tough offensive circumstances, the times when he was one of the only reliable deep options. His release does not erase any of that. But the NFL is not a lifetime achievement award. Teams pay for what a player can do now. Indianapolis judged its roster after three weeks and moved on. Harbaugh had already made that call before the season began, even with a $36 million contract on the books.

And folks, that is the connection to the quarterback decision. Harbaugh moved on from a familiar, expensive veteran because he believed it gave the team a better chance to win now. Reputation did not matter. Contract did not matter. Production mattered. And that same principle should guide what he does at quarterback. Not the fifth-round pick spent on McCarthy. Not the loyalty to Winston for saving the season once already. Just one question. Which quarterback, in which situation, gives the Giants the best chance to win this week? If Harbaugh applies the same standard to the quarterbacks that he applied to Slayton, I trust whatever comes out of this evaluation.

So, let me put all three stories together, because they connect. Slayton’s quick exit from Indianapolis shows how ruthless roster decisions can be, and how Harbaugh is willing to make them. Malachi Fields has the talent to become more than an occasional target, but the Giants have to find ways to use him, especially with tough secondaries coming. And J.J. McCarthy is now at the center of the biggest debate in this building, right as Jameis Winston has given this team reasons to believe in the present. Harbaugh built this roster to win now. Nagy now needs receivers beyond Nabers and Likely, a running game that actually works, and a quarterback plan that adds creativity without sacrificing continuity.

And here is the reality check. The Giants are 3 and 2 and 1, still on top of the NFC East, because nobody else in the division can match that record yet. But the cushion is smaller, and the next game is in Houston against a team Harbaugh says is getting healthy, with a strong secondary. Then comes the bye, then the Eagles, another team known for its secondary. That is exactly the stretch where a predictable offense gets exposed. So in a strange way, the timing of this decision makes sense. If the Giants are going to add a new dimension, now is the time, before the toughest part of the schedule, and with a bye week right after Houston to clean up whatever does not work.

So here is what I will be watching this week. Number one, how Harbaugh talks about the quarterbacks at his midweek press conference. Does he name Winston the starter clearly, or does he keep it open? Number two, the practice reports. If McCarthy is getting more reps with the first team, that tells you the package is real and growing. Number three, the running back competition, because the McCarthy plan only works if one of those backs grabs the job. And number four, Malachi Fields. If he starts showing up more in the game plan, that is the third option this offense needs.

And here is where I stand, because you deserve my honest opinion. I think Harbaugh is making the right call by keeping Winston as the starter while growing McCarthy’s role. Winston has earned the right to keep his job after three good starts and one up-and-down day. But McCarthy has earned more snaps, because he gives this offense a weapon it does not have otherwise, and because the running game desperately needs help. If it works in Houston, the Giants go into the bye at 4 and 2 and 1 with a new dimension that the Eagles have to prepare for. If it does not, Harbaugh has two weeks to fix it. Either way, this is a coach trying to raise the ceiling of his team, not protect his ego. And that is why I still believe what I have been saying all season. This team can go all the way.
