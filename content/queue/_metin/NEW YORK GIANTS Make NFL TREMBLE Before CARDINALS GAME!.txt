Now, go back to the map we drew that week, because the three paths were clear. Path one, free agency, cheapest and weakest, the Garoppolo and Hendon Hooker aisle. Path two, the trade market, with all its dangers. Path three, the sneaky practice squad poach, with the elevation trap waiting inside it. And here is what actually happened, folks. The Giants barely touched path one and path three. They walked straight into the trade market, the most dangerous aisle in the store, the exact place where desperate teams get robbed, and they came out with three quarterbacks without a single franchise-altering scar. Kirk Cousins from Vegas. J.J. McCarthy from Minnesota. Mac Jones from San Francisco. Three different deals, three different profiles, one coherent plan. That alone deserves respect, because the trade lane was the one we feared most.

And now the test that matters, the Sam Bradford test. Remember the cautionary tale we told before any of this happened. Ten years ago the Vikings lost Teddy Bridgewater, the league smelled desperation, and Minnesota paid a first round pick and a fourth for a bridge quarterback. That was the nightmare scenario, the desperation tax, and we begged this front office not to pay it. So let's check the receipts. Kirk Cousins, the leading touchdown passer in the NFL at the time of the deal, cost a second round pick and a conditional fifth. J.J. McCarthy, a former tenth overall selection, cost a 2027 fifth rounder. The Mac Jones compensation has stayed quieter in the reporting, but nobody anywhere has described it as painful. Add the whole basket together, folks, and the Giants rebuilt an entire position room for less than what Minnesota once paid for one Sam Bradford. No first round pick left the building. The future is intact. And how was that possible? Because of the thing we said would matter most. Jameis Winston went out in a monsoon and beat Tennessee 12 to 7, the ship steadied, and Joe Schoen got to shop as a calm buyer instead of a drowning man. Patience created leverage, leverage created prices, and prices created a haul. On the desperation test, this front office gets the highest grade I can give.

Speaking of fit, run the three additions through the profile we wrote before any of them arrived, because this is my favorite part of the report card. We said this offense needs a processor, not a gunslinger. Someone comfortable throwing on the move, comfortable handing the ball off thirty times without sulking, and allergic to the catastrophic mistake, because the identity, power run game, play action, bootlegs, quick clean decisions, was never going to change with the quarterback. Now look at the room. Kirk Cousins is the archetype of that profile, a fifteen year master of throwing on time off play action, the single cleanest stylistic match on the market. J.J. McCarthy literally won a national championship in the college cousin of this exact system, handing it off forty times and striking off the run fake. And Mac Jones made his name as a clean, on-schedule operator who takes what the defense gives. Three for three on scheme fit, folks. Nobody in this room is a hero-ball gunslinger fighting the system. Every one of them is a steady hand who respects the formula. That is not luck. That is a front office that knows exactly who it is.

But a report card has to show the whole transcript, and here is the subject no trade fixed. The pass rush. While the quarterback room was being rebuilt into a palace, the defense lost Brian Burns for the season to that torn ACL, and the edge rush, already the honest weakness of this team, became the single biggest threat to everything else. No quarterback acquisition helps you on third and eight when the opposing passer has four seconds to survey. The interior wall of Dexter Lawrence and Daron Payne is real, and the Dennard Wilson disguise packages bought this group a formula against Tennessee. But Kayvon Thibodeaux's win rate has to rise from the floor, Abdul Carter has to carry a number one's burden, and if this front office's aggressive streak continues, do not be shocked if the next rescue mission targets the edge before the trade deadline. The quarterback crisis is solved, folks. The pass rush question is just getting started, and an honest report card says so in red ink.

And then there is the question that no trade, no press conference, and no report card can answer yet. When does the baton actually pass? Because the beautiful, uncomfortable truth of this roster is that Jameis Winston is 2 and 0 as a starter in games this team had to have, and he is also the man keeping the most accomplished quarterback room in football waiting in line. The referendum continues Sunday against Arizona, and the runway after that, at Washington, home for New Orleans, at Houston, is the softest stretch this schedule will ever offer. If Winston keeps winning, the room stays patient, Cousins keeps absorbing the offense, McCarthy keeps apprenticing, and nobody rushes anything. If the offense keeps averaging field goals instead of touchdowns, the shelf life of belief we talked about starts expiring, and the staff now has answers sitting right there, warmed up and ready. Either way, and this is the point, the Giants win. That is what the rescue mission actually bought, folks. Not a controversy. Options. The most valuable currency in football.

And keep the Winston benchmarks from the Titans week taped to your screen, because they still define the referendum. Zero turnovers, that box keeps getting checked, and it is the biggest reason this team is 2 and 1. Third down conversions back above fifty percent, still waiting. Two or more of those long ten play drives per game, still waiting. Jameis does not need to be spectacular Sunday against Arizona. He needs to be complete. Clean football plus red zone finishes equals job security, even with a hall of fame resume warming up behind him. Anything less, and the most patient quarterback plan in football starts moving on its own schedule.
