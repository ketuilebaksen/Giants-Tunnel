The whole NFL spent this week laughing at what happened in Washington’s final 55 seconds, and while everybody else was pointing fingers, John Harbaugh made a decision that tells you exactly how he plans to run this football team. His kicker missed three field goals in that game. His running back fumbled in overtime with the ball in field goal range. A lot of coaches would have answered that with a panic move, a new kicker on the practice squad, a running back in the doghouse, a quarterback controversy for the cameras. Harbaugh did none of that. Instead, he stood at the podium and said the one thing that matters more than any of it is protecting the football. He called it, and I am quoting him, probably the most important thing with our offense right now. He said the Giants have had way more fumbles than they should, and that they are making a big deal out of it. No scapegoats. No roster shake-up. One standard, out loud, in front of the whole league. Folks, in a week where Washington’s coaching staff became a punchline, that is the bombshell. And today, before the Saints kick off at MetLife, I am going to explain why that decision might be the most important one of the season.

Let me start with the other sideline, because you cannot understand Harbaugh’s decision without understanding what the NFL watched in Washington. Even a week later, analysts are still breaking down that ending, and one of them described it as both teams having a win on a platter and choosing to eat the platter instead of the win. Absolute tomfoolery, he called it. And you know what, from the Giants’ side, I cannot even argue. This was a game New York could have won, and a game New York absolutely should have lost.

Former NFL quarterback Dan Orlovsky walked through the Washington side, and he made a point I had not fully appreciated. He said the problem did not start on the final drive. There were three different drives where Washington had a chance to end the game and did not, the final drive of the fourth quarter, an earlier drive in overtime, and then the final drive. And he said one of the reasons Washington did not finish the first two was the lack of an all-out, zero-blitz plan. Then he explained how the final drive is supposed to work. When an offense gets the ball in that situation, the coaches have a conversation. There is a minimum yard line the team has to reach for the kick to have a chance, and then there is the ideal spot for the kicker. Washington reached that minimum with 55 seconds left on the clock, first and ten, the clock stopped, no timeouts.

And here is the part that should make every coach in the league wince. Orlovsky explained that once a play ends, the 40-second play clock starts, and the coach-to-quarterback communication in the helmet cuts off at 15 seconds. That leaves roughly a 25-second window to tell the quarterback the plan, spike it, throw it away, or get out of bounds, and make sure everybody in the huddle knows it. Instead, Washington took about 42 seconds to get organized and snap the ball. By the time it was snapped, there were only a handful of seconds left, Jayden Daniels did not get rid of it, and the game was over. Orlovsky said he did not know how you go from 55 seconds, with the minimum already accomplished, to wasting 42 of them. And then he said the line that made the whole studio laugh. He thinks they believed another quarter of overtime football was coming. He pointed out that after the final whistle, some Washington players were putting their hands up like it was the start of a new period. Folks, there is no fifth quarter in the regular season. There is a tie, and you take it home with you.

Then Rex Ryan, a former head coach who has been in that headset many times, went even further. He said he did not think we would see something like this again for a hundred years. And he explained what is supposed to happen. As a head coach, even if you are a defensive-minded coach calling the defense, you always keep a button on your headset to switch to the offense. In that situation, you say it plainly. We are spiking the ball, then we are kicking it. And you say it twice, because, as Rex put it, every coach says everything twice. That is just what you do as a coach. Another former player on that panel remembered how Mike Shanahan used to tell his teams that you need 17 to 18 seconds to run two plays in that situation. Once the clock drops below that, everybody on the field should already know the plan without being told. He agreed that the coaches should have told Daniels, but he also said a third-year quarterback should understand that on his own by now.

And then came the most honest take on the panel. It was first and ten with the clock stopped at 55 seconds, already in field goal range. Washington has a defensive-minded head coach, a brand-new offensive coordinator, and a young quarterback. Nobody was excused, because everybody deserves blame when something like this happens. But that analyst said it came down to a lot of inexperienced people, and somebody did not take charge. Another former player said that even in his 16th and final season, he would have expected detailed instructions over the headset immediately after the snap. Are we centering the ball? Are we getting it to a hash? Are we trying to get more yards? And, obviously, we have no timeouts.

Now, I want to be fair to Washington, because both the quarterback and the head coach stood up and owned it. Jayden Daniels said, we got to stop the clock, we got to know the situation, we got to relay that as an offense, it starts from everybody top down, but ultimately, it is on me. And Dan Quinn went even further. He said the sequencing of that does not fall on Daniels. It is either an out-of-bounds throw or a clock play, and that has to come from him. He said he did not have to put his quarterback in that spot, he could have done it earlier and finished with the kick, because they did not need to gain more yards. So, that is on me, he said. Credit to both of them for taking it on the chin. But owning a mistake after the game does not put a single point back on the scoreboard.

And here is why all of this matters for us. That word, somebody did not take charge, is the exact opposite of how John Harbaugh runs a football team. Hold that thought, because we are coming back to it.

First, though, we have to be honest about the Giants’ side of that game, because the Giants were dangerously close to losing everything before Washington unraveled. Let’s go back to overtime. At the two-minute mark, Jameis Winston hands the ball to Cam Skattebo, who had taken his gloves off in the middle of the game, and the ball gets knocked loose. And here is the part that stung. That happened after Dominic Zvada had already missed three field goals, and with the Giants in field goal range, they decided to keep running the football. You could understand the logic, burn the clock, get a little closer, make the kick shorter for a kicker who was struggling. But the ball came out, and the cameras caught Skattebo on the sideline with his head in his hands, because he thought he had just lost the game. Then Washington got into field goal range with a big play down the field, and if they had simply spiked it and kicked it, the Giants walk out of that stadium with a loss, and everybody spends this week talking about the fumble.

So, both teams had their chance to win, and both teams wasted it. One analyst said the Giants players celebrated like they had just won the Super Bowl, because they escaped with a tie. And I get it. When you think you have lost, and suddenly you have not, that is the emotion. But a tie is not a win, and Harbaugh knows it.

That brings me back to the decision. When a team leaves points on the field like that, three missed kicks and a fumble in field goal range in overtime, the easy move is to find somebody to blame. Bring in a few kickers for workouts. Put the running back on the bench for a series to send a message. Make a big show of it. Plenty of fans wanted exactly that this week. And Harbaugh went the other way. No kicker workouts made the transaction wire. Zvada was not cut. Skattebo was not buried. Instead, Harbaugh made the issue itself the headline.

Here is exactly what he said when he was asked about the fumbles, and whether ball security is even something you can coach. Well, you can definitely coach it. It is a major point of emphasis. The league is more of a punch-out league than it has ever been before, and more players are really good at it than they have ever been before. And we better ramp it up just like everybody else in the league. It has been a major point of emphasis from day one, and it is still not good enough. So, we have got to get a lot better in that area. That has probably been the most important thing with our offense right now, but really all the time. The fact that we have had a number of fumbles, way more than we should, we are making a big deal out of it.

Folks, read that again. Not good enough. Way more than we should. The most important thing with our offense right now. He is not hiding the problem, and he is not hanging it on one player either. Remember, the Giants lost two fumbles in Washington, one from Winston and one from Skattebo, and either one of those could have flipped the tie into a win. Harbaugh treated both of them the same way, as a team problem with a team fix. That is the decision. Raise the standard for everyone, instead of making an example of one guy.

And on the kicker, Harbaugh had already explained earlier this week that Zvada’s misses came on a torn-up, muddy field where he could not plant his foot properly, and that the rookie knew what had gone wrong before the game was even over. Think about what Zvada did before that day. He was perfect. He became the first Giants rookie ever to make three field goals of 50 or more yards in a season. An undrafted kid who carried the scoring in the monsoon in Tennessee. One bad day in the mud does not erase that, and Harbaugh is betting that a young kicker who feels trusted by his head coach will kick better than a young kicker who looks over his shoulder at every workout. I agree with that bet. Kickers live in their own heads. The worst thing you can do to a rookie kicker is make him feel like one more miss means he is gone.

Now, let me give you the other side, because I promised you on this channel I would always do that. Some people will say Harbaugh is being too soft. That three missed field goals in a one-score game are a fireable offense for a kicker in this league, and that loyalty does not win in October. And some will say that if the fumbles keep coming, talking about it at the podium means nothing. That is fair. Words are not results. If the Giants put the ball on the ground twice against the Saints today, all of this sounds hollow. But here is my answer. Harbaugh is not saying nothing changes. He is saying the change is in the standard, the drills, the way the ball is carried, and in how much attention every single fumble gets in that building. That is how you fix a problem that belongs to the whole offense instead of one player.

And this is not just a feel-good story. There is a reason to believe ball security can be coached, and Giants fans have seen it with their own eyes. Think back to Tiki Barber. Early in his career, fumbling was the biggest knock on him. When Tom Coughlin arrived in 2004, he changed the way Tiki carried the football, high and tight, against his body, and Tiki went on to put together the best seasons of his career. Same player, same talent, a different standard, and the problem got a lot smaller. That is exactly the kind of fix Harbaugh is talking about. It does not show up in a press release. It shows up in a fourth quarter when the ball stays in a running back’s hands while a defender is punching at it.

And now let me come back to that phrase from the Washington breakdown, somebody did not take charge. Because if there is one thing John Harbaugh’s career is built on, it is taking charge of exactly those moments. Go back to Super Bowl XLVII. The Ravens were up 34 to 29 over the 49ers, backed up deep in their own territory, fourth down, with about 12 seconds left. Instead of punting and risking a block or a return, Harbaugh’s Ravens had punter Sam Koch run around in the end zone and take an intentional safety, burning most of the clock. The 49ers got the free kick with only a few seconds left, and the Ravens walked off as Super Bowl champions. Folks, that is clock management under the brightest lights in the world, planned in advance, communicated to every player on the field, and executed perfectly. That is the opposite of 42 seconds of confusion.

That is the difference I want you to see. Washington had a plan problem. Nobody knew what to do, and by the time somebody figured it out, the clock was at zero. Harbaugh is a coach who has spent his career turning those moments into plans before they ever happen. And yes, the Giants did not handle every moment perfectly in Washington either. Running the ball in field goal range in overtime after three missed kicks was a decision you can debate all day. But the response tells you everything. Washington is still answering questions about why nobody took control. Harbaugh came back to work and took control of the one thing that cost his team the win.

And Giants fans, our history shows us exactly why this matters. Go back to November 1978, the game everybody in this fanbase knows as the Miracle at the Meadowlands. The Giants were leading the Eagles with the ball and seconds to go. All they had to do was kneel on it. Instead, a handoff was botched, the ball hit the ground, Herm Edwards scooped it up and ran it into the end zone, and the Eagles won the game. One play. One end-of-game decision, one ball on the ground. That moment changed this franchise. It led to a front-office overhaul, it helped bring George Young to New York, and eventually Bill Parcells, and two Super Bowl titles. It even changed football itself, because that game is a big reason why the victory formation became standard across the sport. So when Harbaugh says ball security is the most important thing with his offense, this franchise knows better than anybody that one fumble at the end of a game can echo for decades.

Now, let’s talk about where this team stands, because it is worth stepping back and appreciating it. The Giants are 3 and 1 and 1, alone in first place in the NFC East. That is not where a lot of people expected this team to be once Jackson Dart went down. And in that Washington game, they erased a 14-point deficit in the fourth quarter. Jameis Winston threw for 326 yards and three touchdowns. Malik Nabers had 121 receiving yards. Isaiah Likely caught two touchdowns. An offense that looked buried came back swinging like a team that believed it belonged on top of the division.

And here is the stat I keep coming back to. Jameis Winston is unbeaten in his starts this season, two wins and that wild tie. Harbaugh has said the offense is adapting to Winston’s strengths, and you can see it. This is not a backup trying to survive anymore. This is a veteran quarterback giving his teammates a reason to believe that this season still matters. And here is where the ball security decision really comes in. Jameis has always been a gunslinger. He has always been a quarterback who will make the big throw and, sometimes, the big mistake. If the Giants can keep the explosive plays and take away the giveaways, this offense becomes very hard to stop. That is the line Harbaugh is walking. He does not want to take the aggressiveness out of Winston or out of Skattebo. He wants to keep every ounce of that energy and take the ball off the ground.

But I also want to be honest about the brutal reality, because the source of this week’s debate asked a question I think every Giants fan should sit with. Resilience is great, but resilience alone will not keep you in first place forever. So, was that tie the warning that saves this season, or the first sign that this NFC East lead is more fragile than anyone wants to admit? My answer is this.
