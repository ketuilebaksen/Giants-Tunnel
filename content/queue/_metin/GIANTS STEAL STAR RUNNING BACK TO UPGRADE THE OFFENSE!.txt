The Giants just pulled off a steal, and they did not even have to leave their own stadium to do it. Breece Hall is a New York Giant. The explosive running back who spent his career on the other side of the MetLife hallway is walking across to the blue locker room, with Tyrone Tracy Jr. heading the other way in the deal. Folks, think about the timing. One week after the Giants’ running backs managed 21 yards on eleven carries in the first half in Washington. One week after an overtime fumble turned a win into a tie. One week after the whole fan base started asking whether this offense should become a shotgun, spread-it-out attack built around Jameis Winston. Joe Schoen answered with a back who can run inside, bounce outside, and catch the football like a receiver. That is the upgrade. And here is the thing, folks, it is not even the only upgrade happening in this building. Because while the offense just added a star, the defense has quietly transformed itself into one of the most aggressive units in football, and the numbers behind that change are wild. Today we are going to break down both. What Breece Hall brings, why he fits exactly the offense the Giants should be running, and how Dennard Wilson turned a defense that could not get pressure into a blitzing machine. Let’s get into it.

Let’s start with why this move makes so much sense. Go back to the Washington game. In the first half, the Giants tried to be a power running team. Heavy formations, under center, pounding away. And it went nowhere. Eleven carries, 21 yards. The safeties crept up, stacked the box, and dared the Giants to beat them. Then in the second half, the coaches spread the field, went no-huddle, and let Winston throw from the shotgun, and everything changed. The safeties had to back up, and suddenly Cam Skattebo found running lanes in the fourth quarter and overtime. The lesson was clear. This offense, with this quarterback, works best when it is spread out and moving fast. And a spread, up-tempo offense needs a very specific kind of running back. A back who can run from the shotgun, catch passes out of the backfield, split out wide, and turn a checkdown into a twenty-yard gain. That is exactly who Breece Hall is.

Let me tell you about him, for the fans who have not watched him closely. Breece Hall came into the league as a second-round pick out of Iowa State in 2022, and he was electric right away, until he tore his ACL in his rookie season. Sound familiar? Just like Malik Nabers, he had to fight his way back from a major knee injury. And in 2023, he came back and put together a monster year, close to a thousand rushing yards and more than seventy catches. Folks, that is what makes him special. Most running backs are either runners or receivers. Hall is both. He has the speed to hit a home run from anywhere on the field, and the hands to be a real weapon in the passing game. Put him in a shotgun offense with Jameis Winston, Malik Nabers, and Isaiah Likely, and defenses suddenly have four players they have to account for on every snap.

And think about how this changes the matchups. When Washington blitzed, Isaiah Likely was the safety valve in the middle of the field. Now there is a second safety valve, a running back who can leak out of the backfield and make the first defender miss. When defenses roll coverage toward Nabers, Hall gets a linebacker on him in space, and that is a matchup he wins all day. And when defenses drop more players into coverage to stop the passing game, the box gets lighter, and Hall gets to run against six defenders instead of eight. That is how a spread offense is supposed to work. Every piece makes the other pieces better.

Now, what about Cam Skattebo? Because I know some of you are worried this is a message about the fumbles. Here is how I see it. Skattebo is not going anywhere. He is the heartbeat of this offense, the guy who runs angry and drags defenders for extra yards. But he has been playing through a shoulder injury, and he has had ball security issues. Adding Hall does not replace Skattebo, it protects him. Now the Giants can use Skattebo as the thunder, the short-yardage hammer and the closer in the fourth quarter, and use Hall as the lightning, the speed back and receiver out of the shotgun. Fresh legs, fewer hits on a banged-up shoulder, and two completely different styles for defenses to prepare for. And Najee Harris is still in the room as the veteran who can carry the load when needed. Folks, that is a backfield built for a long season.

And there is one more piece of the Hall puzzle that I find really exciting, and it connects to the quarterback story. Remember the package of plays the Giants have built for J.J. McCarthy, the designed runs and zone reads that use his legs? Now imagine that package with Breece Hall in the backfield. McCarthy at quarterback, Hall next to him, the defensive end has to choose. Crash on Hall, and McCarthy keeps it around the edge. Stay home on McCarthy, and Hall gets a crease with his speed. That is the kind of read option that gives defensive coordinators nightmares, because both players can go the distance. Whether McCarthy plays a handful of snaps or eventually more, having a back like Hall makes every one of those snaps more dangerous.

Now let me be fair about the risks, because every trade has them. Hall has to learn a new playbook in the middle of the season, and the running back position is one where blitz pickup and pass protection matter a lot, especially when Winston is sitting in the shotgun waiting for routes to develop. If Hall misses an assignment in protection, that is a hit on the quarterback. So expect the coaches to ease him in, probably as a receiving back and a change-of-pace runner first, before he takes on a full workload. And like any back who has had a major knee injury, the Giants will want to manage his touches smartly over a long season. But the upside is obvious. A player with his speed and hands does not come available very often, especially for a fourth running back.

And yes, it costs the Giants Tyrone Tracy Jr. We have talked about Tracy as a trade candidate on this channel for weeks. He is a good player, a hard worker, and a guy who put up career-high special teams snaps this season. But in a four-man running back room, somebody was always going to be the odd man out. Turning a fourth running back into a star-level weapon is exactly the kind of move a first-place team makes. And the fact that Joe Schoen did it with a team that shares his own stadium makes it even better. Trades between the Giants and Jets are rare, folks. These two franchises share a building, a city, and a fan base that argues about everything. So when a star crosses the hallway, it is a big deal, and Giants fans get to enjoy it every time Hall breaks a long run in the same building where he used to wear green. This front office has not stopped all season. Daron Payne, the quarterback frenzy, Joey Porter Jr., Jadeveon Clowney, the Yannick Ngakoue workout, and now Breece Hall. Every week, Schoen finds another way to make this roster better.

Now, here is the part a lot of people are missing. While the offense was getting its upgrade, the defense already went through its own transformation. And honestly, folks, I think we all had the wrong expectations about this defense coming into the season. With Abdul Carter and Kayvon Thibodeaux leading the front, everybody expected the Giants to get serious pressure without blitzing. Win with four, drop seven, let the front do the work. That clearly has not worked out the way we imagined. This defense entered the Washington game with the fewest sacks in the entire NFL. But Dennard Wilson made a major adjustment, and it is starting to reveal what this defense is actually capable of.

Look at the numbers, because they tell the whole story. Against Arizona, the Giants blitzed on 54 percent of opposing dropbacks. Against Washington, it climbed to 62 percent. And in the fourth quarter and overtime against Washington, it hit 74 percent. The Giants ranked second in the entire league in blitz rate that week. That is a massive shift. Instead of occasionally sending an extra defender to surprise the offense, the Giants are now making pressure a central part of who they are. Wilson looked at a plan that was not producing results and changed it, in the middle of the season, and that is exactly what good coordinators do.

And here is my favorite part of this story. The change started with the players. During the game, Deonte Banks and Greg Newsome went to Dennard Wilson and volunteered to play man-to-man coverage. Think about what that means, folks. Blitzing always comes with risk. When you send extra rushers, the defenders left behind have to hold up in coverage with less help. A coordinator cannot blitz like that unless he trusts his corners. And here were two corners walking up to their coordinator and saying, put us on an island, we will hold up. That is confidence. That is what Deonte Banks winning NFC Defensive Player of the Week does for a player. And it gave Wilson the green light to get aggressive.

One player who has thrived in this new approach is Andru Phillips. He has been making an impact as the nickel and slot corner, especially when the Giants send him on blitzes. He got to the quarterback twice against Washington. And there was one play that showed exactly why this works. Jayden Daniels was focused on Abdul Carter and Arvell Reese coming off the same side, and Phillips came flying in from the left and got to him first. That is how the threat of one defender creates an opening for another. The quarterback has to identify the pressure, figure out where his protection is weak, and make a decision before the rush arrives. When the Giants keep showing different looks, those decisions get harder and harder. Now, Phillips has been solid against the run, but his pass coverage is still a concern, so using him as a blitzer in the right moments plays to his strengths.

And folks, this is why I keep telling you not to judge the defensive line only by sack totals. Abdul Carter and Arvell Reese can change a play just by forcing the quarterback to worry about them, even when someone else gets home. Pressure can mean forcing the quarterback off his first read, disrupting his timing, or making him react before he wants to. That said, the blitz has to complement the pass rush, not become an excuse for the front four to disappear. And Carter is not disappearing. He had a sack and a forced fumble in Washington, chasing down Daniels from behind after beating the left tackle.

Kayvon Thibodeaux is a more complicated story. His sack and pressure numbers have not been where he wants them to be, and with his contract situation, there is real pressure on him to deliver. But Thibodeaux has been doing a solid job setting the edge against the run, keeping running backs from finding easy lanes outside. And he made one of the biggest plays of the Washington game on that wild final sequence, taking away the tight end and the sideline and forcing Daniels back into the middle, where he got sacked as time ran out. The Giants need him to keep that edge discipline and add more impact as a pass rusher.

Then there is Darius Alexander, and his story is one I really like. He was a healthy scratch after a rough start to the season. But he got another chance, and since returning, he has contributed against the run and helped generate pressure on the quarterback. Players do not always establish themselves immediately, and a tough opening stretch does not have to define a season. If Alexander keeps improving, the Giants have another defender who can rotate in and contribute in different situations.

Now, not every position is developing that smoothly, and safety is the biggest concern. Tyler Nubin has been up and down, flashing good run defense but also making mistakes that lead to big plays. He missed a tackle on Washington’s opening touchdown run, and taking a bad angle can undo an otherwise great defensive snap. Dennard Wilson wanted to build on what Nubin did well in college, but it has not consistently worked out. The other options are Jason Pinnock, who has been in the concussion protocol, and Ar’Darius Washington, who made some impressive plays in the last game and looks like he deserves more snaps. The competition next to Jevon Holland is wide open. And here is why it matters so much. When you blitz this often, the players behind the blitz have to make the right decisions in coverage. One mistake by a safety can turn into the kind of big play that wipes out all the good work happening up front.

So how aggressive should the Giants be going forward? My answer is somewhere around 45 to 55 percent blitz rate, depending on the matchup and what the offense is showing. That keeps the pressure on without becoming predictable. They do not need to blitz on three out of every four snaps every week. The goal is to make pressure a regular weapon, then adjust based on the opponent. Some games will call for more aggression, others for more patience. If Wilson makes those calls well, offenses will never know what is coming.

And there is some history behind this, too. Back in the 2025 season, when Shane Bowen was let go as defensive coordinator and Charlie Bullen stepped in as the interim, the Giants defense reportedly became more aggressive and started getting better results. That is a good reminder that this roster responds when it is unleashed. The Giants need a scheme that fits their players instead of sticking to one approach no matter what. And in 2026, Dennard Wilson seems to have figured that out.

And credit to the offense as well, because the Washington game showed real growth after some adjustments. Jameis Winston got the offense going once the scheme was built around his strengths, and Matt Nagy deserves credit for adapting the game plan in the middle of the game, even though there are still plenty of issues to fix. And now, with Breece Hall joining the backfield, Nagy has an even better tool to build that shotgun offense around.

And think about how these two upgrades connect, because that is what makes this week so exciting. An aggressive, blitzing defense creates quick stops, short fields, and turnovers. A spread offense with Winston, Nabers, Likely, and now Hall can turn those short fields into touchdowns fast. When your defense is forcing three-and-outs and your offense can score in four plays, you play with a lead. And when you play with a lead, Carter and the pass rush get to pin their ears back even more. That is the cycle every great team wants. Defense creates chances, offense cashes them in, and the lead feeds the pass rush. For the first time this season, the Giants have the pieces to run that cycle every single week.

And against the Saints, we will get the first real look at it. A home crowd at MetLife, a team that has not lost there all season, Jameis Winston facing his old franchise, Breece Hall making his Giants debut, and Dennard Wilson’s blitz packages waiting for a Saints offense that has shown real flaws. Folks, I cannot wait for Sunday.

So let’s put it all together, folks. The Giants are 3 and 1 and 1, alone in first place in the NFC East, with two fewer losses than anyone in the division. The offense just added a star running back who fits exactly the style that works best with Winston. The defense has transformed from a unit that could not generate pressure into one of the most aggressive blitzing teams in the league, with corners who volunteer for man coverage and a nickel who gets home off the edge. And the next opponent is the New Orleans Saints at MetLife, where the Giants have not lost all season. That is the perfect stage for Breece Hall’s Giants debut and for this new aggressive defense to show what it can do in front of the home crowd.

I said it before, and I am saying it louder now. This team can go all the way this season. The front office keeps adding talent. The coaches keep adapting. And the players keep believing. When all three of those things are happening at the same time, special seasons happen.
