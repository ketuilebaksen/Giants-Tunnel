Dose number one: Zvada gets another chance.

Let’s start with the decision that has New York holding its breath. One missed kick, three points left on the field, a 24-21 loss to Washington. And John Harbaugh is not hitting the panic button. According to reporting from Locked On Giants, the team has not scheduled a tryout to challenge the rookie. For now, the message is clear. Dominic Zvada is the kicker, and he is getting another opportunity to prove he can handle the job.

Here is the math, and it is a little uncomfortable. Zvada has converted 17 of 23 field-goal attempts this season. That is just under 74 percent. But from 50 yards and beyond, he has made six of nine. That is two-thirds of his long-distance tries. So he clearly has the leg to give his coaches confidence from way outside normal range. What he has not shown consistently is the accuracy to make the routine kicks feel routine. And at this stage of the season, reliability matters more than the highlight of a 55-yard bomb.

Now, why do I call this good news? Because of how it was handled. Patricia Traina from Locked On Giants offered a possible explanation for the slump. She raised concerns about Zvada’s plant foot and his hip mechanics, and she suggested confidence may be playing a role too. Those are her observations, not a confirmed diagnosis. But they line up with what Harbaugh himself said this week, that there is a technique issue with the plant foot and that all of the misses have come from the right hash. When the coach and the people watching the tape are seeing the same thing, that means the problem has been identified. And an identified problem is a fixable problem.

Kicking is all about precise timing. A tiny change in where the plant foot lands, or how the hips rotate through the ball, can change the path of a kick that has to split a narrow target. And then there is the mental side. When a kicker misses one he expects to make, every kick after that can start to feel heavier. Harbaugh has to decide whether giving Zvada space to fix his motion will bring his confidence back, or whether more misses will make it worse. By not bringing in a tryout, Harbaugh is betting on the first one. He is telling his rookie, we believe in you, go fix it.

Traina also made a really important point that I want to repeat. The Washington loss cannot be put on Zvada alone. There were dropped passes. There were protection breakdowns. There were a lot of wasted opportunities. Three points never tell the whole story. She is right. But acknowledging all of that does not erase the responsibility of a kicker asked to make a 33-yarder.

So here is the real test. Patience only works if it produces progress. Zvada does not have to make every kick from impossible distances. He has to make the ones his teammates fought to set up. And he has to make them when a miss would hurt the most.

Picture it. A close fourth quarter at MetLife on Sunday against Jacksonville. The Giants grind their way into range. Zvada jogs onto the field. Nobody is thinking about his longest make. Everyone is wondering if he can finish the drive. One clean kick in that moment could quiet all of the noise. Another costly miss would make Harbaugh’s patience a lot harder to defend.

My opinion, not a report: I like the decision. Changing kickers in the middle of a division race, with a guy who has made six from 50-plus, is a huge risk too. You could easily bring in a veteran who does not know your snapper and holder and miss a kick anyway. Harbaugh came up as a special teams coach, and he is trusting his eyes. I am trusting him for one more week.

Dose number two: the Giants get their chance to prove it, and the offense is ready.

Okay, now let’s face the number the critics love. The Giants are leading the NFC East, and that should be cause for celebration. But there is a number hiding underneath those standings that changes the conversation. What if the Giants’ biggest achievement so far is simply beating the teams they were supposed to beat?

Here is the analysis. ESPN’s Football Power Index ranks the Giants’ schedule so far as the 31st toughest in the league. Only one team in the NFL has had an easier path. And here is the second number. The Giants have played only one team that currently has a winning record, the Los Angeles Rams, who are 6-4. And the Giants lost that game by 22 points, the same game where Jackson Dart got hurt. The combined record of the teams the Giants have beaten is 13 wins and 34 losses.

Now, those numbers do not erase the wins. A win is a win, and the Giants did not make their schedule. But they do limit what we can conclude. And we have seen this story before. The 2020 Steelers looked unstoppable early and then lost their first playoff game. The 2023 Eagles looked headed for another deep run and then collapsed down the stretch. Different teams, same warning. A good record in November is not a playoff win in January.

So why is this good news? Because the next stretch is the chance to answer all of it. The difficulty is about to flip. The Giants’ remaining strength of schedule ranks seventh toughest in the NFL and toughest in the NFC East. Five of their next six opponents have winning records. Jacksonville, San Francisco, Detroit, Seattle. These are not games where the Giants can count on the other team to make the bigger mistakes.

And I love that. Because the critics have one argument left, that this team has not beaten anybody good. Well, here come the good teams. Win a couple of these, and that argument is gone forever. A single statement win against a real contender changes the whole national conversation. Beat one heavyweight, and the Giants have proof that this system works when the difficulty rises. Lose all of them, and the critics will say the division lead was built on soft ground. That is the tension. The standings say the Giants belong. The proof is right in front of them.

Now, let me push back on the soft-schedule argument a little, because I think it is only half the story. Yes, the teams the Giants have beaten have a combined losing record right now. But some of those teams did not look like losing teams when the Giants played them. Think about the 19-11 win in Philadelphia. That was the first Giants win there since 2013, snapping a 13-game losing streak that included the January 2023 playoff loss. The Eagles’ record now makes that look smaller, but winning in that building has been one of the hardest things in the NFL for this franchise for over a decade. And the Houston win was one of the most complete performances of the season, on the road. The Giants also have the week one win over Dallas, 28-20. Some of these opponents have fallen apart since then, and part of the reason is that the Giants beat them.

There is another thing the strength-of-schedule number does not capture. The Giants have done all of this with three different starting quarterbacks. Jackson Dart started the season, then went down against the Rams. Jameis Winston stepped in. Then J.J. McCarthy took over. They also lost Brian Burns for the season in week three. A team that keeps winning through that kind of turnover is not just riding a soft schedule. It is a team with a real foundation.

So I will meet the critics halfway. The schedule has been soft, and the Giants have not yet beaten a team with a winning record. That is a fair point. But the way to answer it is simple. Beat Jacksonville. And here is what a statement win would look like to me. The defense holds a 7-2 team that just scored 40 points on Tennessee to under 20. The offense finishes drives with touchdowns instead of field goals. Special teams wins the field-position battle instead of losing it. And in the fourth quarter, if it is close, the Giants close. Do that, and nobody will be talking about the 31st-ranked schedule on Monday.

And here is why I think they can do it. The offense is finally developing in the right places.

J.J. McCarthy has built real chemistry with Malik Nabers and Isaiah Likely. That gives the passing game multiple ways to attack. Likely has been on fire, with a career high in catches in Philadelphia and then nine catches for 127 yards and a 66-yard touchdown against Washington. McCarthy just threw for a career-high 293 yards. And Cam Skattebo just delivered his two strongest games of the season, including 78 rushing yards and two touchdowns against Washington, plus 44 more receiving.

If that progress continues, the Giants can do what good teams do against good opponents. Control the clock. Protect the quarterback. Make defenses deal with more than one weapon. That is exactly how you beat a better team.

The questions are real, though. Can McCarthy stay composed when the pocket collapses? Against Washington’s heavy pressure, he struggled, two for six for 11 yards and an interception against six or seven rushers. Can Nabers create separation when defenses give him extra attention? Can Skattebo keep moving the chains when the running lanes close up against better fronts? Those are the questions the next six games will answer. And I think the answers are going to be better than people expect. My opinion, not a report.

Dose number three: a quiet signing that could swing close games.

There is a type of NFL signing that barely makes a headline but can decide a close game. And I think the Giants just made exactly that kind of signing.

The Giants signed Tavierre Thomas from the Minnesota Vikings’ practice squad, according to reporting attributed to Adam Schefter. Thomas has played 116 NFL games for Cleveland, Houston, Tampa Bay and Minnesota. But the most important part of his résumé may not be his work in the secondary at all. In 2025, Thomas led the Vikings with 19 special teams tackles. Nineteen. That is a guy who lives on the kicking units.

ESPN’s Jordan Raanan suggested Thomas could help the Giants specifically as a gunner on punts. And this is where it gets important. A gunner is the guy who sprints down the sideline on a punt to make the first hit on the returner. Get there fast and tackle cleanly, and an ordinary punt becomes a field-position weapon. Miss the tackle, and suddenly the other team is working with a short field.

And that is exactly where the Giants have been vulnerable. Nick Jones and Elijah Campbell have handled the gunner duties, but both have missed tackles and committed penalties. Their special teams grades from Pro Football Focus sit below 60, according to the reported analysis. That is not good enough for a team that wins close, low-scoring games. Thomas gives that unit another option, a veteran who has built a career on doing those little details right. He raises the floor of a unit that has had too many avoidable breakdowns.

There is also an interesting defensive connection. Thomas is coming from Minnesota, where Brian Flores has built one of the most aggressive defenses in the league. And Giants defensive coordinator Dennard Wilson has increased his own use of pressure packages since Brian Burns went down. So Thomas already knows what it means to play in an aggressive, blitz-heavy scheme. He has handled slot coverage before, and he could be an emergency option there if needed.

And the secondary has reasons to value an extra experienced player. Tyler Nubin has faced criticism for inconsistent play. Ar’Darius Washington has played better recently, including that interception of Jayden Daniels late in the fourth quarter on Thursday, but he has a history of injuries. Depth matters in December. Still, I want to be clear. Thomas’ clearest path to playing time right away is on kick coverage, not as a defensive savior.

And the timing matters too. Harbaugh built his reputation partly through special teams, and yet the Giants’ third phase has struggled this year. The team just brought Xavier Gipson back as a return option after Charlie Jones’ ball security problems, two lost fumbles on returns this season. Now Thomas is being asked to help on the other side of those exchanges. One guy to return. One guy to cover.

Think about the bigger effect. A clean tackle at the 15-yard line instead of the 35 forces the other offense to earn 20 more yards. One disciplined coverage snap changes the plays an offense calls and the risks its quarterback has to take. Against Jacksonville, San Francisco or Detroit, those extra yards could be the difference between surviving a close game and chasing another frustrating loss. For a team about to face tougher opponents, preventing one long return can be just as valuable as one explosive play on offense.

And do not underestimate what this does for the defense too. When the coverage units pin the opponent deep, this defense gets to play the way it wants to play. Opponents backed up near their own goal line get conservative. They run the ball. They throw short. And that is when Arvell Reese and this pass rush can pin their ears back on third-and-long.

So those are your three doses. Harbaugh trusts his young kicker and the problem has been identified. The Giants get their chance to prove they belong, with an offense that is clicking. And a special teams veteran arrives to fix a quiet leak.

Now let me put it all together. Big Blue, the pressure is building. Zvada has to turn trust into made kicks. Harbaugh has to prove these Giants can beat strong opponents. And Thomas and Gipson have to bring discipline to special teams. That is a lot riding on one Sunday. But it is also a huge opportunity. Because the beauty of a two-game lead is that the Giants can play this game free. A win is a statement. A loss is a setback, not a disaster. That is a great position to swing big from.
