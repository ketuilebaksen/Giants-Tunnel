Story number one, and it is the one guaranteed to light up every comment section in New York, because somebody just put J.J. McCarthy's name next to Eli Manning's. There is a phrase this fan base reacts to differently than almost anything else in sports, and that phrase is Eli Manning. Two Super Bowls. Two Super Bowl MVPs. Two legendary runs that nobody saw coming. A quarterback who survived one of the ugliest beginnings a top pick ever had in this market and came out the other side permanently welded to the identity of this franchise. So when McCarthy arrived in New York and longtime New York columnist Gary Myers placed his first ten starts next to Eli's first ten starts, the comparison was always going to explode. And here is the part that surprised people, the raw numbers are genuinely comparable, because Eli's first ten starts were rough, folks, really rough, and McCarthy's ten starts in Minnesota, the 57 percent completion rate, the eleven touchdowns against twelve picks, sit in the same messy neighborhood. The numbers say two young quarterbacks drowning early. One of them became a legend.

But before Big Blue turns that into a prophecy, let me do what this tunnel always does and separate the hope from the evidence, because Myers himself was careful here. He explicitly said he was not predicting anything close to Manning's accomplishments. His point was simpler and smarter, teams can give up on quarterbacks too quickly, and sometimes development arrives later than the draft slot promised. That is fair, and quarterback history backs him up, the league is full of players who needed a different environment, a different voice, or simply more time before it clicked. But now the warning, because the context of these two situations could not be more different, and context matters far more than the numbers. Eli Manning came to New York as the anointed future. The franchise traded up for him on draft night, Kurt Warner was openly the bridge, and every person in that building knew exactly what the long-term plan was. The organization was committed to surviving his growing pains because there was no alternative plan. McCarthy arrives in the opposite universe. He is a reclamation bet acquired for a 2027 fifth round pick after his original team had already moved in a different direction. He begins behind Jameis Winston on the depth chart. And the franchise quarterback this organization has actually invested its heart in, Jackson Dart, is rehabbing down the hall. Eli had a runway with no exits. McCarthy has a tryout with many.

And there are two more layers to the warning that I need you to sit with, because they are the strongest arguments for caution. Layer one, the durability file. McCarthy lost his entire rookie season to a knee injury, and his ability to stay on the field became part of the conversation in Minnesota, fair or not. Layer two, and this is the big one, the way Minnesota moved on. When the Vikings went out and acquired Kyler Murray, the message became impossible to ignore. You do not bring in a player capable of taking the job if you believe in the man holding it, you bring in a traditional backup. And here is the detail that makes it sting more, Kevin O'Connell had continuity with McCarthy. This was not a young quarterback abandoned after three coordinators and two regime changes, which is the usual sad story. The front office changed, yes, but the head coach had been there the whole time, saw every practice, sat in every meeting, had direct exposure to McCarthy's development, and that staff still chose to move on. When the people with the most information walk away, folks, you have to at least respect the information.

So where does that leave us? Exactly where the smart money should be, and it is why I love this trade anyway. The Giants paid a fifth round pick, which means this is precisely the kind of calculated gamble a well-run team should take every single time. If it hits, you have found a 23 year old quarterback with first round pedigree, a national championship, and a locker room captain's resume, for pocket change. If it misses, the cost is a day three pick and nobody remembers. And the New York situation hands McCarthy the one thing Minnesota never could, time without a coronation. He does not arrive as the savior, Winston is ahead of him, Dart owns the future, and nobody is demanding anything from him in October. All he has to be is a player willing to rebuild his value and prove Minnesota pulled the plug too early. That is the healthiest possible assignment for a career reset. But here is where it gets fascinating, folks, because the quiet scenario is sitting right there. What happens if Winston struggles through this soft stretch of schedule? What happens if McCarthy, with his teammates already raving about the strong arm and the fast learning, picks up Matt Nagy's system quicker than expected? Suddenly the conversation stops being theoretical, and if that man gets on the field and flashes, every old Vikings decision, every quote, and yes, every Eli comparison comes roaring back at full volume. Comment reset if you think New York is the perfect place for McCarthy to rebuild, comment caution if you think expectations should stay buried until he proves it on grass. I genuinely want the room split on this one.

And since the honest framing is judge McCarthy on his own trajectory, not on Eli's memory, let me give you the actual markers to watch, so we evaluate like scouts instead of fans. Marker one, command. When McCarthy gets practice reps or mop-up snaps, watch whether the operation is clean, the huddle, the cadence, the protections, because command is the first thing that shows when a quarterback truly owns an offense, and the first thing missing when he does not. Marker two, the middle of the field. His best throws at Michigan and his best moments in Minnesota came attacking between the numbers off play action, which happens to be exactly where this Giants offense lives, so if the fit is real, it shows up there first. Marker three, availability, the boring one that decides everything, a full healthy season of practices would do more for his stock than any highlight. And marker four, what the coaches do, not what they say, because if that scout team assignment quietly turns into first team reps during a bye week or a blowout, the staff will have told us their evaluation without a single press conference. Track those four things with me this season, folks, and we will know what the Giants have long before the league does.

Story number two, and this one is emotional, because the Odell Beckham Jr. chapter is officially closed, and the full accounting is more brutal than the headline. We covered the release when it happened, but now all the details are out, and they paint the complete picture. The reunion everybody wanted to believe in lasted exactly three games. Twenty offensive snaps. One target. Zero catches. Read those numbers again, folks. That is not a limited role, that is barely a role at all. One of the most electric receivers in the history of this franchise came home, and the football simply never found him. And the timing of how it ended still does not sit cleanly, so let's be honest about the sequence. Days before the Tennessee game, Harbaugh stood at the podium and said he would be all for Beckham having a bigger role, we covered that quote in this very tunnel and got excited about it with you. Then Sunday came, and Beckham played six offensive snaps with one target. The fans at MetLife could feel it too, because during that 12 to 7 rock fight you could hear the chants rolling down from the stands, O-B-J, O-B-J, a whole stadium trying to will the reunion into existence. And days later, he was released. From a bigger role is coming, to six snaps, to gone, inside one week.

Now, here is the part that I genuinely respect, even inside the disappointment. When Harbaugh explained the decision, he did something coaches almost never do. He pointed the finger at himself. He said the Giants simply were not getting enough from the situation, that he wanted Beckham to do more, wanted to put him on the field more, and that the staff could not make it happen. And then the line that matters, put it on us. No anonymous leaks about conditioning. No whispers about route running decline. No making a legend the villain on his way out the door. The head coach stood up and said the failure belonged to the staff. And he went further, this was nothing about Odell, nothing Beckham could have done differently, their relationship has not changed, he coached the man in Baltimore, considers him a great friend, and hopes he lands somewhere else and produces. Folks, you can disagree with the roster decision and still recognize what that is, that is leadership, and in a league where exits usually come wrapped in anonymous character assassination, put it on us is the most honorable sentence available.

But the honest analysis cuts both ways, so let's ask the uncomfortable questions. If Harbaugh truly wanted more from Beckham, why did the offense never build him a real test? Why only twenty snaps across three games? Why one single target? Why publicly suggest a bigger role and then cut the man almost immediately after? Even if releasing him was ultimately the correct football decision, and I believe it was, the path to that decision leaves legitimate room for criticism. And then there is the other interpretation, the one I keep coming back to. Maybe the staff already had its answer, and they got it where fans never look, at practice. Coaches see the separation, the burst, the conditioning, the timing, every single day. We see sixty game snaps a week, they see six hundred practice reps. If the evaluation in those practice reps said the other receivers gave this offense more, then a sentimental roster spot was never going to survive long under a regime still establishing its standards. Look at the room they chose instead, Nabers as the alpha, Darnell Mooney stretching the field, and the practice squad promotions of Braxton Berrios and Charlie Jones, both of whom feed the special teams machine we broke down this week, a phase where Beckham offered nothing. That is the cold arithmetic of the modern NFL, folks. The one-handed catch against Dallas lives forever in the highlight reels, and this fan base will love Odell Beckham Jr. until the end of time. But legacy buys you a tribute video, not a roster spot. Comment OBJ if you think he deserved a real opportunity before the door closed, comment roster if you think Harbaugh chose correctly between nostalgia and utility, and hit like if you respect a coach who says put it on us instead of burying the player.

And that decision points directly at story number three, because if reputation could not protect Odell Beckham Jr., then nobody on the margins of this roster should feel safe, and there is one name the logic keeps circling. Tyrone Tracy Jr. might be the next Giant to watch before the November 10 trade deadline. And folks, the strange thing is not that Tracy could be traded. The strange thing, after the month this front office just had, is that he is still here. Think about where his season stands. Before the year, the vision was Tracy and Cam Skattebo as something close to a 1A and 1B backfield pairing. That vision faded in the preseason opener when Tracy missed a key block, and it disappeared completely when Najee Harris arrived and leapfrogged him on the depth chart. Against Tennessee, his entire offensive contribution was a single three yard gain. Meanwhile, his special teams snaps have climbed to a career high 41, and his most visible moments this season were two punt returns of 26 yards each in the Rams game. Read that trajectory honestly, folks. When a running back's value migrates from the backfield to the return unit, the offense has already told you what it thinks. Add the fumbles that have dogged him, add the fact that he turns 27 in November, an age where running backs stop getting developmental patience, and the picture sharpens.

Now, why Miami specifically? Because the fit practically draws itself. The Dolphins just lost De'Von Achane to a torn ACL, months after guaranteeing him 32 million dollars. They are sitting at 0 and 3 with their season slipping away. And the depth behind Achane is exactly why the idea has legs, Jaylen Wright has battled injuries going back to college, and Ollie Gordon has averaged a grim 2.7 yards per carry since entering the league. A desperate team with a hole at running back, and a buried back in New York with proven return skills and multi-purpose experience, that is how late round picks change hands in October. And from the Giants' side, the question is brutally simple, does Tracy have any meaningful offensive future here? Skattebo is the present, Najee owns the complementary role, and the long-term picture is not even settled behind them, because Skattebo turns 25 in February with two seasons left on his deal, which means this staff could easily revisit the position in the next draft with its own hand-picked profile. If the coaching staff no longer sees Tracy as part of the offense, holding him purely because he is under contract through next season creates no value. The smart posture is the one I suspect Joe Schoen already holds, listen to everything. If somebody calls offering real draft capital, even a late rounder, for a player your own depth chart has marginalized, that is a call you take. And remember the deadline mechanics, folks, November 10 is a hard wall. Once that clock expires, the option simply disappears, and this front office has shown us five times in a month that it does not like letting options expire. Comment trade Tracy if you would take a reasonable pick right now, comment keep him if the depth and special teams value matter more to you.
