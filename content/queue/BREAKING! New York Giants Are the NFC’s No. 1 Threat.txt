Is Arvell Reese the closest thing to Micah Parsons the Giants have ever had?

Let me start with that stat, because it is incredible. Four or more sacks, an interception, a forced fumble, and a fumble recovery, all in your first eight career games. In the last 25 seasons, only two players have checked every one of those boxes. Nick Bosa, who became a Defensive Player of the Year and a Super Bowl pass rusher in San Francisco. And Arvell Reese. Eight games into his career, Reese is already sitting next to Bosa in the record books.

And one of the analysts who broke it down could not help himself. He said the Giants have struck gold with Reese. He said Reese is already in the conversation for best middle linebacker in football. He put Fred Warner at number one, and said there might be another guy or two he was missing, but that Reese is in the combo. And then he said this. When we start to see the pass rusher in him, you go, holy crap, maybe we do have Micah Parsons. Maybe we do. He is a difference maker. He was all over the field.

Now, I want to be careful with comparisons like that. Parsons and Bosa have years of elite production. Reese has eight games. But the comparison is not about the numbers. It is about the kind of player he is. A linebacker who can play the run, cover, and rush the passer at a high level. That is rare. And it changes how an offense has to play against you.

Let me show you why, because the film breakdowns on Reese this week were fantastic. One former coach pointed to a play that did not even end in a sack, and said it might be the most important play of the game. The Eagles ran play action with Joe Milton under center, faking the handoff to Saquon Barkley. Reese read run and came all the way to the line of scrimmage. That is what he is supposed to do. He has to respect the run. But then, when he recognized it was a pass, he slammed on the brakes, got depth, and got underneath both crossing routes, right where Milton wanted to throw. And Milton had nowhere to go. Reese took the throw away.

Think about how hard that is. A rookie linebacker biting on play action, then recovering in time to cover two receivers crossing behind him. Most linebackers get caught. Reese did not. That is speed, but it is also eyes. The analyst said Reese is seeing everything in front of him, passing receivers off, and has his eyes in the right place. That is not normal for a rookie.

And remember, Reese was drafted fifth overall this year as an inside linebacker. Joe Schoen said during the bye that the clear vision for Reese was to play off the ball, but the Giants wanted to see if he could also play on the edge. When Brian Burns went down, Dennard Wilson started using Reese as a pass rusher too. Now he is doing both at a level we rarely see from any player, let alone a rookie. And John Harbaugh said after the game that he and Schoen were giddy when Reese fell to them. He called Reese humble, tough, coachable, and a quiet leader. Harbaugh gave him the first game ball and said he could have given him two or three.

And there was another play that showed off his burst. The Eagles tried to cut Reese off on the backside with left guard Landon Dickerson, a Pro Bowl lineman. The analyst said Dickerson can cut off just about any linebacker in the league. But not Reese. Reese took a great angle, beat the block, and chased down Barkley. That is the burst that makes the Parsons comparison make sense.

And then there was the forced fumble. According to the breakdown, Joe Milton had 2.29 seconds before a rookie linebacker knocked the ball out of his hands. Reese came off the edge untouched, hit Milton so hard he said it might have been the hardest hit of his life, and Micah McFadden recovered the ball. That erased a Charlie Jones muffed punt and changed the momentum of the game.

Now, let’s talk about the scheme, because Dennard Wilson deserves a lot of credit. One of the analysts explained how the Giants used simulated pressure in this game. That is when the defense lines up showing an all-out blitz, with a lot of players at the line of scrimmage. The idea is to scare the offense into keeping extra blockers in to protect the quarterback. And then, at the snap, the defense drops players back into coverage. On one play, the Eagles packed in extra protection, and the Giants dropped eight into coverage against just three Eagles receivers. Milton had nowhere to go.

The analyst said that is how you win on third down and second and long. If you can get the quarterback, the offensive coordinator, and the offensive line coach to max protect and limit the number of options, you are winning that down before the ball is even snapped. You could almost hear the Eagles’ play caller going through his sheet, crossing out answers.

And this is where Reese makes the whole thing work. Because he can rush the passer, the offense has to respect the blitz. But because he can cover, the defense can drop him and still take away a throw. You cannot tell what he is going to do before the snap. That is exactly why the Parsons comparison has teeth. Reese can force you to protect against a blitz and still take away the pass.

And I want to give some love to Kayvon Thibodeaux, because one of the analysts made a great point. Thibodeaux does not have big sack numbers this season. But he is playing really hard and doing a lot of little things. Chucking a tight end at the line so he does not get a free release. Getting steady pressure. And the analyst pointed out that DJ Reader’s sack in this game really came from Thibodeaux. Thibodeaux beat the right tackle inside, and even though he missed the sack, he forced Milton to step up right into the arms of Reader. The Giants are also moving Thibodeaux all over the formation, over the guard, out wide, and he is controlling the line of scrimmage with his hands.

That reminds me of what Brian Burns said a few weeks ago. He called Thibodeaux the unsung hero of this defense. And he said people judge pass rushers only by sack numbers, when you have to watch the tape to see the impact. On Sunday, the tape showed it again.

And this is what makes the Giants’ defense so hard to block. You have Reese, who can rush or cover. You have Abdul Carter, who Burns called unblockable at times. You have Thibodeaux, doing the dirty work and moving all over the line. And you have DJ Reader in the middle, finishing plays when the quarterback steps up. When all four of those players are working together, the quarterback has nowhere to go. Forward is Reader. Outside is Carter and Thibodeaux. And in the middle of the field, waiting, is Reese.

And here is a fun stat about the sacks. According to one of the analysts, the Giants and Eagles came into this game near the bottom of the league in sacks. The Eagles had 10, and the Giants had nine. Then in this game, the Giants got seven and the Eagles got six. Pass rushers always say sacks come in bunches, and that is exactly what happened. Now, I want to point out that the Giants ranked near the top of the league in pressure earlier this season, so the sacks were always going to come eventually. On Sunday, they finally did.

Health decides it, not hot hands.

If McCarthy is healthy, he should play the whole game. If he gets hurt again, Winston is ready. But you do not want your starter looking over his shoulder every time he has a bad series. Now, here is the challenge on Thursday, and it is a huge one. Jayden Daniels. Washington’s quarterback is one of the fastest players in the league at his position. If you want to spy Daniels in certain situations, you need a player who is as fast as he is. And on the Giants, the only player as fast as Daniels is Reese.

So Dennard Wilson has a decision to make. Do you use Reese as a pass rusher, where he just had three sacks? So put yourself on the Giants’ headset. It is third and seven. Do you send Reese after Daniels and risk opening a running lane? And if Reese can run down Daniels in open space, every contender in the NFC is going to have to ask what happens when its quarterback runs out of the pocket and runs straight into him.

And remember, the Giants and Washington tied 27 to 27 in week five. Washington is 2 and 5 and 1 after a 20 to 13 loss to the Rams. But Daniels is the kind of player who can win a game by himself. That is why the rush lanes matter so much. The edge rushers cannot all crash inside. Somebody has to keep contain. And if Daniels does get out, Reese has to be there to close the door.
