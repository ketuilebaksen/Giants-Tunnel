The Giants just hit the jackpot, and it came in three pieces. A veteran edge rusher just landed in the building, but it was not the name everybody expected. A head coach just told the world he is willing to bend his offense to fit his quarterback, and the numbers behind that decision are some of the most striking I have seen all season. And a special teams unit that was one of the worst in football last year is now sitting third in the entire NFL. Folks, none of those headlines is flashy on its own. But put them side by side, and they describe a team that is quietly learning how to win. And I think the middle one, the quarterback and the scheme, might matter most of all. Today I am going to break down all three, the surprise signing, the quarter-by-quarter numbers that prove Jameis Winston is a different quarterback when the offense is built for him, and why special teams might be the hidden reason the Giants are sitting in first place while teams like the Rams and the Eagles are falling apart. Let’s get into it.

Let’s start with the move that surprised me. Earlier this week, we told you the Giants were bringing in Yannick Ngakoue for a workout. A former Pro Bowler with 71 and a half career sacks, a player who sat out last year to get fully healthy, and a guy who has played for John Harbaugh before. A lot of us, me included, assumed he was coming in. Instead, the Giants signed Dawuane Smoot to the practice squad.

So who is Dawuane Smoot? He most recently played for Jacksonville. He is 31 years old, with 27 career sacks across nine seasons. He is listed around six-foot-three and 275 pounds, and the word on him around the league is that he plays with a high motor. He played all 17 games for the Jaguars last season, and he has 127 career games under his belt. He is a power player with a strong first step.

But here is what I like first, and it is not even Smoot himself. It is the process. Every time the Giants add depth this season, they bring in several players instead of just one, and they compare them on the field. They do not fall in love with a name. And folks, Ngakoue made all the sense in the world on paper. He knows the system. He knows Harbaugh. Plenty of people wondered whether a fully healthy Ngakoue could unlock an older version of himself. So think about what it means that the Giants worked him out, and then chose someone else. If you bring in a player who has been in your coach’s building and understands exactly what you want, and you still pick a different player, that other player must have shown you something. Maybe it was burst. Maybe it was movement. Maybe it was how quickly he picked up the details. I cannot tell you exactly what it was. But I can tell you that choosing Smoot over a familiar name tells me the staff trusted what it saw with its own eyes, not reputation. That is how good organizations make decisions.

And there is another smart angle here. Practice squad signings are low stakes by design. The Giants do not have to commit a roster spot or a big contract to find out what Smoot can do. If he looks sharp in practice, they can elevate him on game days. That keeps the decision-making based on performance, and it keeps the next move available if injuries force one. Folks, this front office has been aggressive all season, Daron Payne, the quarterback frenzy, Joey Porter Jr., Jadeveon Clowney, Breece Hall. But it is also being careful at the margins. That is a good balance.

Now, let’s be realistic about what Smoot is not. He is not an every-down edge rusher. Nobody is signing him so they can bench Kayvon Thibodeaux and start Smoot every week. Last season, he had two sacks, five quarterback hits, and 18 total pressures. If he had been dominant, he would probably be on a roster right now. But that is not the job. The job is depth. And I wonder about his flexibility, too. He can play outside linebacker in a 3-4 front, but at around 275 pounds, he might also be big enough to slide inside to defensive end in that same front. That matters, because Chauncey Golston just went to injured reserve with his neck injury, Brian Burns is out for the year, Abdul Carter has been dealing with a calf issue, and Thibodeaux could use an occasional breather.

And folks, depth like this tends to matter more than people think, and it usually shows up in November rather than October. Pass rushers get hurt. They get tired. They lose snaps to conditioning. A veteran who knows his assignments can fill a rotation spot without the defense having to simplify its calls. He will understand when to set the edge, when to rush, and when to drop into coverage. He is not going to make rookie mistakes. That is the unglamorous value of a player like Smoot. And with Dennard Wilson blitzing more than almost any coordinator in the league, having bodies who understand assignments matters even more. I would much rather have him on the practice squad than not have him at all.

Now, the story I am most excited about. The quarterback and the scheme. Think about what we all went through watching that Washington game. In the second quarter, Jameis Winston had that terrible fumble, and every Giants fan in the country immediately thought, it is J.J. McCarthy time. Then in the second half, he started slinging it, played excellent football, and led a comeback. By the end, plenty of people were thinking, maybe Jameis is the guy, but only if the offense is built around him.

And that has been my argument from the moment the game ended. The Giants have to decide what they want to be. If they want an under-center, play-action, bootleg, run-heavy, pro-style offense with a mobile quarterback, then McCarthy is the better fit, and they should make the switch when he is ready. But if they want Jameis, they have to give him the shotgun and tempo. What they cannot do is ask Winston to run an offense that does not suit him, because we have now seen some very ugly first halves followed by second halves where necessity forces him to play his own way.

What bothered me about those first halves was not just the results. It was the way the offense seemed to be working against its own quarterback. When a player is uncomfortable with the mechanics of a play, everything slows down by a fraction of a second. And in the NFL, a fraction of a second is the difference between a completion and a sack. Winston is at his best when he can see the field quickly and throw on rhythm. Asking him to take a drop from under center, turn his back to the defense, and wait for a bootleg to develop takes that away from him.

And John Harbaugh actually addressed it. When he was asked about the tempo, he said it was good, no question, and that the staff had been working on it, calling it part of the system from the beginning. He admitted they may have gotten to it because the game forced them to, but he said that was fine, because it opened up the offense and showed how effective it could be. And his conclusion was that it probably needs to be a bigger part of what they do going forward. And earlier this week, he went even further, admitting that it was tough and awkward for Winston to jump into an offense built for a different quarterback, and that they are now getting reps on more Jameis-type things.

Folks, I love hearing that. Harbaugh is not a coach who locks into one idea and tells his quarterback to live with it. He is looking at what Winston does well and letting Jameis be Jameis, instead of forcing him to run what was designed for Jackson Dart or what McCarthy might do. That is the sign of a coach secure enough to adapt. And it takes humility. A coach who spent months building an offense around one idea has to be willing to say the idea might not be the best version for the player in front of him. Plenty of coaches cannot do that. They protect the scheme and blame the execution. Harbaugh’s willingness to say tempo needs to be part of the plan, without any defensiveness, tells me he cares more about winning than about being right. And honestly, it is one of the main reasons I think he is among the best coaches in the business.

Now, here are the numbers, and they are striking. In the first quarter against Washington, Winston went 4 of 8 for 46 yards, with an expected points added per pass of roughly zero. Basically neutral. In the second quarter, he went 3 of 8 for 26 yards, slightly negative, and that is when the fumble happened. Then the Giants fell behind, the offense changed, and everything flipped. In the third quarter, he went 8 of 11 for 70 yards, at about plus 0.63 expected points per pass. In the fourth quarter, he went 13 of 17 for 101 yards, at about plus 0.74 per pass. Overtime was not as pretty, but he still put the team in position to score until Cam Skattebo’s fumble.

Pay attention to the size of that swing, folks. Going from roughly neutral to more than half a point per pass, in the same game, with the same quarterback, against the same defense, is the kind of change you almost never see unless the scheme itself is the variable. The players did not suddenly get better in the third quarter. The offense simply gave them a better way to play.

And when you zoom out to the whole season, the same pattern shows up. Now, the sample is only five games, so I would not treat it as a law of nature. But the direction is hard to ignore. Winston has been poor in first quarters and even worse in second quarters, because those are the stretches when the Giants lean on the under-center approach. Then in the third quarter, once the game demands it, he has posted one of the best marks of any quarterback in the league, around plus 0.45 expected points per pass. That is not a coincidence, folks. That is a road map.

Now, there is one reason I think Harbaugh might hesitate to go fully up-tempo. This offense was designed to possess the football. We saw it clearly in week one, when the Giants kept Dallas off the field and won with ball control. And up-tempo football can mean shorter drives and less rest for your defense. So I checked what has actually happened. Even with tempo against Washington, the Giants held the ball about nine more minutes than the Commanders. Against Tennessee, they were almost nine minutes ahead in possession. Against Arizona, about four and a half. That is not the domination of the Dallas game, but controlling possession in any form is still a win. So the fear that tempo will kill time of possession has not shown up in the numbers.

And there is a defensive payoff, too. It is hard to play defense when you are trailing, because the other team has its whole playbook available. But when the opponent falls behind and has to throw, that is when you can dial up blitzes and hunt for turnovers. Think about it from Dennard Wilson’s point of view. A defense playing with a lead can call games that a defense playing from behind cannot. Extra rushers, bracketed coverage, creative disguises, all of those become safer when the opponent has to throw. And turnovers tend to come when offenses are forced into hurried decisions. If tempo helps the Giants score earlier, the defense gets to play its preferred style for longer stretches of the game. That is the cycle I want to see. Score early, build a lead, let the defense attack.

Now, the third piece of good news, and it is more of a statistic, but it might be the most underrated story on this team. John Harbaugh built his entire career on special teams. He started as a special teams coordinator, and when he got to New York, he promised to rebuild that unit. And through the first five weeks, the Giants rank third in the NFL in total special teams expected points added. Last year, they were among the worst in the league. Folks, flipping that in a single season is a testament to what a coach can change when he treats a phase of the game as a priority. And here is a fun bit of history for you. Before he ever became a head coach, John Harbaugh spent years as the special teams coordinator for, of all teams, the Philadelphia Eagles. He built his reputation in that city by understanding that the third phase wins close games. And now, in his first season with the Giants, his special teams are part of the reason the Giants are sitting above the Eagles in the standings, while Philadelphia just lost a game on a kick coverage breakdown. Folks, you cannot make this stuff up. When the Eagles let Harbaugh walk out the door all those years ago to go coach the Ravens, they had no idea he would one day show up in East Rutherford and outclass them in the very phase of the game he used to coach for them. Remember what Phil Simms told us a couple of weeks ago, that when Harbaugh comes into meetings, the first thing he talks about is special teams, special teams, special teams. This is the result.

And yes, the Giants were even higher earlier in the season, before Dominic Zvada’s rough day in the mud in Washington. But even after three missed kicks on a torn-up field, they are still third in the entire league. That tells you how good the rest of the unit has been. Jordan Stout booming 70-yard punts. Coverage teams flying down the field. And Theo Johnson, a starting tight end who had zero special teams snaps last season, now running people over on kickoff coverage.

And folks, you cannot overstate how much special teams matters. Look around the league this past Sunday. The Los Angeles Rams cut their kicker right after an overtime blocked field goal, even though it did not feel like it was entirely his fault. Sean McVay said afterward that the unit was not good enough. The Rams are last in the league on special teams, and one of the most talented teams in football is sitting at 2 and 3, largely because of it. On the other side, the Minnesota Vikings have had one of the weaker offenses in the league, but a strong defense and elite special teams have them at 5 and 0.

And then there is Philadelphia, which is the example that should make every Giants fan smile. The Eagles grade well overall, but their kick coverage against Jacksonville was awful. They gave up a return for a touchdown that was called back by a penalty. And then, after taking the lead with eight seconds left, they kicked off to the Jaguars and gave up a return of 50 or 60 yards, which helped set up Jacksonville’s winning score. Everyone ripped the decision to kick it, but you should be able to trust your coverage unit to make a tackle in that spot. That one special teams moment cost the Eagles a win, dropped them to 2 and 3, and helped the Giants extend their lead in the NFC East.

The reason special teams gets overlooked is that its impact is invisible until it fails. A good kick return might save 15 yards of field position, and nobody notices. A bad coverage unit gives up 40, and everybody does. But over a season, those hidden yards add up to points, and points add up to wins. Teams that prioritize the third phase are quietly building an advantage that does not show up on the highlight reels. And the Giants, under Harbaugh, are one of those teams.

So let’s put all three pieces together. A front office that evaluates players on the field instead of by reputation, and builds depth carefully. A head coach willing to bend his offense to fit his quarterback, backed by numbers that show Winston is a completely different player in the shotgun and in tempo. And a special teams unit that went from one of the worst in football to third in the league in a single season. That is a team that is quietly learning how to win.

And the timing could not be better, folks. The Giants are 3 and 1 and 1, alone in first place in the NFC East, and a struggling Saints team is coming to MetLife on Sunday. If the Giants come out in tempo, let Winston be Winston, let the defense attack with a lead, and keep winning the hidden yards on special teams, I think they win this game and keep building their cushion before the trip to Houston and the bye week.

I said it before, and the evidence keeps piling up. This team can go all the way this season. Not because of one superstar, but because the organization is doing a lot of small things right at the same time.
