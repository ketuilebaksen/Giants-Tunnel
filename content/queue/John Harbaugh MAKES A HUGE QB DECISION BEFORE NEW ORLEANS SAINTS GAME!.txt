John Harbaugh has made a huge quarterback decision before the Saints game, and it is not the one most people were expecting. Everybody spent the last two weeks asking who should play quarterback for the New York Giants. Jameis Winston or J.J. McCarthy. But Harbaugh just answered a completely different question, and I think it is the more important one. Not who plays. How they play. The offense that was built in the offseason for Jackson Dart, the under-center, bootleg, run-pass option system, is being reshaped around the quarterback who is actually on the field. Harbaugh admitted it was awkward for Winston to play in an offense built for someone else. He said the staff is now getting reps on Jameis-type things. He said the tempo that saved the Washington game probably needs to be a bigger part of what they do going forward. And at the same time, he told reporters that what we have seen from McCarthy so far is probably not all they have for him. Folks, that is a head coach deciding to build the offense around his starter while sharpening a weapon behind him. Today I am going to break down exactly what that decision means, why the numbers forced it, what changes on Sunday against New Orleans, and why Harbaugh has made this exact kind of call before, with a result that changed an entire franchise. Let’s get into it.

Because honestly, it is a little bit of both.

Let’s start with why this decision matters so much, because I think a lot of people are missing it. When a quarterback struggles, the easy move is to change the quarterback. When an offense struggles, the hard move is to change the offense. Coaches build their systems over months. They install them in the spring, rep them all summer, and fall in love with them. Walking away from that plan in October feels like admitting you got something wrong. Most coaches will not do it. They protect the scheme and blame the player. John Harbaugh is doing the opposite. He is looking at the evidence and saying, the player is fine, the fit is wrong, so we will fix the fit.

And he said it out loud. At the podium this week, Harbaugh said it is tough to just jump in there when the offense is built probably for a different quarterback. He said Winston did a really good job, but it was really tough, it was awkward, and he fought through it, kept his confidence, kept his poise, and found a way to win football games. And then came the key line. Now we are starting to get adapted a little bit to the game plan, and getting reps on the things that are more Jameis-type things. So I think we are going to keep getting better. That is the plan. Folks, when a head coach uses the word awkward about his own offense, and then tells you the plan is to change it, that is a decision. That is the huge quarterback decision. Not a benching. A rebuild around the man under center.

And when Harbaugh was asked whether this is the coaches adapting to Winston or Winston adapting to the coaches, he said probably both are required. But then he said something really important. A player is kind of what he is. You cannot turn a pocket passer into a bootleg artist by October. You can only put him in the best position to succeed. And Harbaugh added that Winston is probably more versatile than people give him credit for, joking that Jameis told the team in a meeting, I have always been a scrambler. Folks, a coach who understands what his quarterback is, and builds around it, is a coach who wins games.

Now let’s talk about the evidence that forced this decision, because the numbers are impossible to ignore. Against Washington, in the first quarter, with the Giants running the original under-center plan, Winston went 4 of 8 for 46 yards, roughly neutral in expected points per pass. In the second quarter, 3 of 8 for 26 yards, slightly negative, with a costly fumble. Then the Giants fell behind, the coaches switched to shotgun and tempo out of necessity, and everything flipped. Third quarter, 8 of 11 for 70 yards, around plus 0.63 expected points per pass. Fourth quarter, 13 of 17 for 101 yards, around plus 0.74. Same quarterback. Same defense. Same weather. The only thing that changed was the offense around him.

And it is not just one game. Across the season, Winston has struggled in first and second quarters, the stretches when the Giants lean on the under-center approach, and then posted one of the best third-quarter marks of any quarterback in the league, around plus 0.45 expected points per pass, once the game forces the offense to open up. Now, five games is a small sample, and I would never call it a law of nature. But it is a road map, and Harbaugh clearly read it. And remember the other numbers from Washington. Winston became the first Giant since 1950 to account for 300 passing yards, three touchdown passes, and 25 rushing yards in the same game. He became the first Giant since Eli Manning in 2016 with back-to-back three-touchdown games. All of that came after the switch.

So what actually changes on Sunday against New Orleans? Here is what I expect to see. First, a lot more shotgun. Winston sees the field better from the gun, he gets the ball out on rhythm, and he does not have to turn his back to the defense on play-action fakes. Second, tempo, especially early. Harbaugh said tempo has been in the system from day one and needs to be a bigger part of what they do. I would not be surprised if the Giants script the opening drive at full speed to set the tone against a Saints defense that has been searching for confidence. Third, a quick passing game built around Malik Nabers and Isaiah Likely, who just became only the second receiver and tight end pair in NFL history to each have eleven catches, 90 yards, and a touchdown in the same game, joining Justin Jefferson and T.J. Hockenson. And fourth, Breece Hall. A running back who catches the ball like a receiver is a perfect fit for a shotgun offense. He gives Winston another quick target and keeps the run game alive out of spread formations.

And let me paint a picture of what Jameis-type things actually look like, because Winston’s career gives us a clear answer. Go back to 2019 in Tampa Bay under Bruce Arians, the coach whose motto was no risk it, no biscuit. That was a vertical, shotgun-heavy passing attack, and Winston led the entire league with more than 5,000 passing yards. Yes, the interceptions came with it, but the production was undeniable. That is Jameis at his most comfortable. Quick reads, deep shots, the ball out of his hand before the pass rush gets home. In this Giants offense, that means quick-game concepts like slants and spacing routes to get him into rhythm early, deep shots to Nabers off straight drop-backs instead of slow-developing bootlegs, run-pass options from the shotgun where he reads one defender and fires, and screens to Breece Hall to punish aggressive blitzes. None of that requires Winston to be a different player. It just requires the offense to stop asking him to be one.

And the good news is the play caller is perfect for this. Matt Nagy grew up in the Andy Reid coaching tree in Kansas City, the same tree that turned shotgun spread concepts and creative quick passing into one of the best offenses of the last decade. Nagy coached quarterbacks there, he called plays there, and as a head coach in Chicago, he ran plenty of shotgun and run-pass option looks. So this is not a coordinator being forced into something unfamiliar. This is a coordinator getting to lean on a part of his playbook he knows extremely well. Harbaugh brought the physical, under-center vision. Nagy brings the spread expertise. And now the two are being blended around the quarterback who is actually playing. That is how good staffs are supposed to work.

And here is the part people worry about. Does a shotgun, tempo offense abandon the physical identity Harbaugh wanted? I do not think so. Even with tempo against Washington, the Giants still held the ball about nine more minutes than the Commanders. The run game can still be physical out of the shotgun, with Cam Skattebo as the hammer and Hall as the lightning. And when the Giants get a lead, they can slow down, huddle up, and grind the clock. The decision is not to become a pure spread team. It is to stop forcing Winston into a system that fights against his strengths.

Now, the second half of Harbaugh’s quarterback decision. J.J. McCarthy. When he was asked about the benefits of McCarthy’s package, Harbaugh said it is a change of pace, and then he added, what you saw is probably not all we have for him right now. Whether he is out there will be determined based on whatever happens, but we will see if it can help us. And then he gave the line that explains his entire philosophy. He said, what is the benefit of not doing everything you can to use all the guys you have? We would be negligent if we did not try to do that.

Folks, read that carefully. Harbaugh is not saying McCarthy is about to take the job. He is saying McCarthy is a weapon, and leaving a weapon in the drawer would be negligent. So picture how the two pieces fit together. Winston runs the shotgun, tempo offense that matches his strengths. And in specific moments, short yardage, the red zone, a change of pace after a long drive, McCarthy jogs in and runs the things Winston cannot. Zone read with Breece Hall. Designed quarterback runs. Bootlegs that actually work, because McCarthy has the legs to make them work. That is not a quarterback controversy. That is a coaching staff using each quarterback for exactly what he does best.

And let me explain why the red zone and short yardage are the perfect places for a McCarthy package, because it comes down to simple math. When the quarterback is not a threat to run, the defense can match every blocker with a defender and still have an extra man to tackle the running back. But when the quarterback is a real threat to keep the ball, the defense has to account for him too, and suddenly the offense has an extra player in the numbers game. Near the goal line, where the field is compressed and every yard is a fight, that extra man is everything. A third and one, a fourth and goal from the two, a two-point conversion. Those are the moments where McCarthy’s legs could turn a field goal into a touchdown or a punt into a fresh set of downs. And remember the tie in Washington. The Giants left points on the field with missed kicks and stalled drives. A few extra short-yardage conversions in that game, and we are talking about 4 and 1 instead of 3 and 1 and 1.

And think about how much that complicates life for the Saints. They have to prepare for a gunslinger in the shotgun pushing the ball to Nabers and Likely at tempo. And they have to prepare for a mobile quarterback running read option with an explosive back. Two completely different sets of run fits, pass rush lanes, and coverage rules. A defense that is already 1 and 4 and struggling for answers now has to split its practice time between two offenses. That is exactly the kind of edge Harbaugh loves.

And folks, here is why I trust Harbaugh to pull this off. He has done it before. Go back to 2018 in Baltimore. Midway through the season, the Ravens were struggling with Joe Flacco, the quarterback who had won them a Super Bowl. And when Flacco got hurt, Harbaugh turned to a rookie named Lamar Jackson. But he did not just plug Lamar into Flacco’s offense. He rebuilt the entire offense around Lamar’s legs, in the middle of the season. The Ravens went on a run, won the division, and that decision launched one of the most explosive offenses in modern NFL history. Folks, a coach who has already rebuilt an offense midseason around a quarterback’s strengths and won with it is exactly the coach you want making this call now. Harbaugh is not guessing. He has walked this road before, and he knows exactly how it ends when you get it right.

And there is one more piece to this decision that does not get enough attention. Brian Hudson will start at center again on Sunday. Harbaugh said Hudson played well in both of his starts, and that John Michael Schmitz is still in the plans, but for this week, the job belongs to Hudson. Why does that connect to the quarterback decision? Because in a tempo offense, the center is the one making the calls and getting the line set before the defense can adjust. There is no time for confusion. Keeping the center who was on the field when the tempo offense took off in Washington is part of the same plan. Chemistry between the quarterback and the center matters more when the snaps are coming fast.

And think about what this decision means for Jameis Winston personally. He is 32 years old. He has been a starter, a backup, and a starter again. He knows that every week he plays well in this offense is a week that builds his value, whether that is in New York or somewhere else down the road. When a head coach publicly says, we are going to build more of the offense around what you do best, that is a massive vote of confidence. And confident quarterbacks play better. Winston has already said he is heating up and getting better every week. Now he gets an offense designed to let him prove it. Folks, I would not be surprised if the best version of Jameis Winston we have seen as a Giant is still ahead of us.

Now let’s be honest about the risks, because there are real ones. The biggest is protection. A shotgun, drop-back offense needs a clean pocket, and right now the offensive line is banged up. Right tackle Jermaine Eluemunor left the Washington game with a calf injury, and Andrew Thomas has been dealing with a groin issue and missed Wednesday’s practice. If the Giants are thin at tackle, the quick passing game becomes even more important, because Winston cannot hold the ball. The second risk is ball security. Harbaugh called the fumbles a major point of emphasis and said they are still not good enough. Tempo offenses run more plays, and more plays mean more chances to put the ball on the ground. The Giants lost two fumbles in Washington, one from Winston and one from Skattebo. That has to be cleaned up. And the third risk is the defense. If a tempo offense goes three-and-out quickly, the defense is right back on the field, and this defense is dealing with injuries too, with Abdul Carter missing practice with a calf issue and Tyler Nubin and Colton Hood banged up in the secondary. Smart tempo is selective tempo.

And then there is the opponent, because the Saints present their own test. Harbaugh called them a very hungry, very talented, very well-coached team, and he pointed to the connection between young quarterback Tyler Shough and his top receiver. Do not let the 1 and 4 record fool you. A team with nothing to lose can be dangerous. But a struggling defense against an offense that suddenly fits its quarterback is exactly the matchup you want. And for one more storyline, Drew Brees will be calling the game for FOX, the Hall of Famer whose quarterback room Winston shared in 2020, the same room where Sean Payton used Taysom Hill in special packages next to his starter. Winston saw that model up close. Now he gets to be the starter in a version of it, against Brees’ old team, with Brees in the booth.

So let me give you my verdict on Harbaugh’s decision. I love it. It is honest, because it admits the original plan did not fit. It is smart, because it follows the evidence. It is flexible, because it keeps McCarthy as a real weapon instead of a backup collecting dust. And it fits everything we know about John Harbaugh, a coach who rebuilt an offense around Lamar Jackson midseason and turned it into a juggernaut. The Giants are 3 and 1 and 1, alone in first place in the NFC East, with the Eagles and Cowboys both at 2 and 3 and Washington at 1 and 3 and 1. If this new version of the offense shows up from the first snap on Sunday instead of only after the Giants fall behind, I think the Giants beat the Saints and head to Houston with real momentum before the bye.

And I will say it one more time. This team can go all the way this season. Not because everything has gone right, but because the head coach keeps making the right adjustments when things go wrong.
