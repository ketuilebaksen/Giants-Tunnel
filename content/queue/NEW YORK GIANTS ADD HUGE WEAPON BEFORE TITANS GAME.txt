Let's start with the update that kicked all of this off, because our own injury report is telling a story nobody wants to read. Deonte Banks has now missed practice for a second straight day with a calf issue, and he is sitting there listed as questionable. And that is a real conversation on its own. Now, there has been a lot of chatter about whether a calf injury like this could be connected to an Achilles problem, so let me calm that down, because from everything I understand, those are two pretty different animals. A calf tightening up is not the same as an Achilles injury, which is more of a repetitive stress thing that builds over years, not something that snaps out of nowhere because of one bad step. So I would not panic on that front. But here is the honest part. A calf issue can absolutely limit how explosive a corner is in coverage, and a corner playing at ninety percent against NFL receivers is a corner getting picked on. Stack that on top of the Paulson Adebo situation, because he is already done for the year, and suddenly this position group is walking a tightrope.

Now, on Adebo, let me give you a take that might surprise you. I do not think losing him is the gut punch some people are making it out to be. When we are being honest with each other, this defensive backfield was already considered the weakest position group on the roster coming into the season, and Adebo was not exactly locking receivers down on a weekly basis before he went down. It stings, sure. Losing bodies always stings. But losing a guy who was part of the question is not the same as losing a proven answer, and I do not think it changes the ceiling of this defense the way it is being talked about. What it absolutely does change is the depth math. Because if Banks cannot go, the pressure lands squarely on Colton Hood and Greg Newsome, and if things get truly thin back there, you could see snaps going to Nico Reed, Elijah Campbell, Nick Jones, even Darius Washington, guys most fans could not pick out of a lineup. Against the veteran passers waiting on this schedule, that is not the depth chart you want to be running out there, folks. That is a problem you fix before it becomes a catastrophe.

Which brings us to the conversation that has been building in the background all week, and it ties directly into that bigger question I opened with. Should the Giants make a move for a cornerback? Because there is a very interesting situation developing in Pittsburgh with Joey Porter Junior. By all accounts, things have gotten tense enough between him and the Steelers that they might genuinely take less than full value just to move on. And when you look at what this kid has actually put on tape, you understand why a front office like ours should be picking up the phone. Over the last three seasons, we are talking about basically one touchdown allowed in coverage in that entire stretch. One. At a premium position. At the exact spot where our roster is bleeding. And look, I get why some fans clutch their draft picks like family heirlooms, so let me hit you with the actual math. Somewhere around 45 to 50 percent of first round picks are not even with their original team by year four, and close to half get classified as busts by the very people making these decisions. A draft pick is a lottery ticket, folks. A proven twenty-something corner with elite coverage numbers is a winning ticket somebody else already scratched. And if you need a reminder of what happens when this franchise gets cute about proven defensive backs, think back to Julian Love. We let that man walk instead of paying up, and all he did was go win a Super Bowl and stack All-Pro seasons somewhere else. I am not saying mortgage the future. I am saying when a position of desperate need meets a motivated seller, you at least make the call.

And think about what a corner like that actually unlocks schematically, because this is bigger than one roster spot. Everything we have talked about all season, the pass rush being the heartbeat of this defense, the hits on the quarterback, the collapse-the-pocket philosophy, all of it depends on the coverage holding up for just two and a half seconds. When your corners cannot survive one-on-one, you start doubling, and when you start doubling, you rush four with no help behind it or you blitz and pray. A legitimate press corner changes that entire equation. He lets you play man on one side and roll every safety resource toward the other. He lets Carter and Thibodeaux pin their ears back knowing a receiver is not running free behind them. And in this division, folks, look at who you have to cover twice a year. CeeDee Lamb and Pickens in Dallas. A.J. Brown in Philadelphia. Terry McLaurin in Washington. You do not survive six division games a year with a secondary held together by tape and hope. That is why this conversation matters beyond this week.

Alright, now flip to the offense, because there is genuinely exciting news in that backfield. Najee Harris is in the mix, folks, and I think that is the piece that shifts how this offense attacks the next month. You already know the blueprint this team wants to run. We watched it bury Dallas. It was never about the passing game winning fireworks contests. It was about control. Wearing a defense down through the second half and taking the air right out of the football game. Now add a true power back rotation to that identity. Najee and Skattebo taking turns running downhill behind Pancake Pat Ricard, behind an offensive line that wants to maul people, that is not finesse football, that is imposing your will on strangers for sixty minutes. And that pairing does something else that matters more right now than ever. It protects the quarterback. Whoever is taking the snaps, and for the next stretch that is Jameis Winston, a two-headed power run game means he does not have to carry anything alone. Hand it off, stay on schedule, let the backs and the line drain the clock and demoralize the front seven. Remember the Dallas time of possession story. The first half was close, New York up by only about a minute and a half. Then the second half happened and this team controlled the clock for over twelve extra minutes, leaving Dallas exactly two possessions the rest of the way. That was not an accident, folks. That was the game plan working exactly as designed, and with another power back in the building, there is no reason to ever drift away from it again.

And let me get specific about what Najee actually adds, because this is not just a bigger pile of carries. Skattebo is the sledgehammer, we know that, the guy who turns every run into a personal argument. But sledgehammers swing hardest when they get to rest. A genuine two-back rotation means fresh legs in the fourth quarter, when this offense does its best work, when the clock-draining drives happen, when defenses are gassed and every carry falls forward for an extra yard. It means real answers in short yardage and at the goal line, where a veteran power back who has converted those downs his whole career earns his money. And it means the play action stays honest on every snap, because there is never a moment where the backfield stops being a threat. Teams spent last week daring this offense to run into stacked boxes with one tired back. Good luck stacking boxes for sixty minutes against two of them behind Ricard. That is the counterpunch, folks, and it arrived at the perfect time.

And while we are being honest about the offense, I need to clear up something about Jackson Dart, because the perception machine has been running wild since the injury, and the film tells a different story than the narrative. People out there acting like this offense was smoke and mirrors before the knee, listen to what the tape actually says. Against Dallas, roughly 19 of his 29 throws came from inside the pocket. This was not a backyard scramble show. And when he did leave the pocket on rollouts, the accuracy traveled with him. He was making full progressions, folks. There was a red zone snap where he worked through four separate reads, four, before finding his man near the goal line. Ask yourself, when is the last time you watched a Giants quarterback calmly get to his fourth read under pressure inside the twenty? That is the player rehabbing that knee right now. That is what is coming back to this huddle. And in the meantime, that same pocket foundation is the model for Winston. Nobody needs a hero back there. They need on-schedule football, and the structure to produce it is already installed. One more name to watch as the play action opens back up, by the way. Theo Johnson. A tight end who can work the middle of the field is exactly what this offense needs when defenses start selling out against the power run, and after the rough moment in Los Angeles, I promise you nobody in that building wants a redemption arc more than Theo. Watch for it.

Quick word on the defensive front, because the philosophy conversation matters going forward. Against the pocket passers on this schedule, the plan is not complicated. You do not need to contain guys like that, you need to collapse the pocket and make them uncomfortable on every single dropback. And here is the part I want you to internalize. Hits matter more than sacks. Three clean sacks look great on a stat sheet, but it is the constant pounding, the ten quarterback hits, that rewires how a veteran processes the game in the fourth quarter. Decisions speed up. Mistakes multiply. With Brian Burns on the mend, that duty falls to Abdul Carter and Kayvon Thibodeaux off the edges, with Dexter Lawrence and Daron Payne doing the dirty work inside, and honestly, that interior duo pushing the pocket into a quarterback's lap might be the fastest path to turnovers this defense has.

One more injury-report note from around the league that shapes how I see the market, by the way. These reports move for a reason. When you watch a star's designation flip between doubtful and questionable in the same week, like we have seen with big names across the NFL lately, that is a team telling you it genuinely does not know, not a coach playing games. Front offices read those tea leaves the same way we do, and rosters dealing with real uncertainty get aggressive. That is the environment our front office is operating in right now, and it is exactly why I think the phones around the league are busier than the headlines suggest.
