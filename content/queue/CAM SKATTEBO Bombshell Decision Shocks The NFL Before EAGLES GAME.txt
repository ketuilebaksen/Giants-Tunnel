Cam Skattebo has made his decision, and he is not backing down. After drawing an unnecessary roughness flag in Houston for lowering his helmet into Texans safety M.J. Stewart, a lot of people expected the second-year running back to say the right things and promise to clean it up. Instead, he said this. If I am going to keep getting flags or fined for it, I guess I am going to play for free. Folks, that is a running back telling the entire league that he is not changing who he is. And here is the part that makes this even bigger. His head coach is standing right next to him. John Harbaugh used the bye week to defend Skattebo publicly, and he made an argument that a lot of coaches around the league have probably been thinking for years. A running back cannot just offer his chest to a tackler, Harbaugh said. His head cannot be separated from his shoulders. Harbaugh wants the NFL to tell him exactly what a running back is supposed to do. Today, I am going to break down the play, the rule, Skattebo’s decision, Harbaugh’s argument, and what it all means heading into Philadelphia. Let’s get into it.

Is Skattebo right to keep running the same way, even if it costs him?

Let’s start with the play itself. It happened early, in the first quarter of the 27 to 21 overtime win in Houston, a game the Giants needed badly. Skattebo lowered his head as he carried the ball into contact with M.J. Stewart, and the officials threw a flag for unnecessary roughness. Their concern was the position of his helmet at the moment of impact. And that one call has followed the Giants all the way into their bye week.

Harbaugh’s view is that Skattebo was trying to lead with his shoulder, not his helmet. And what he wants from the league is clarity. He wants to know exactly what a running back is supposed to do when he is running into contact in a tight space and the collision is unavoidable. Think about it from the running back’s point of view. A runner has to lower his pad level to protect himself and to win the collision. He cannot just stand up straight and absorb the hit. If he does, he gets folded in half, and he probably gets hurt. So, where is the line?

Let me explain the rules, because this is where it gets complicated. Back in 2013, the NFL put in a rule that stops ball carriers from using the crown of the helmet, the very top of the head, to initiate forcible contact with a defender when they are outside the area near the line of scrimmage, often called the tackle box. Then, in 2018, the league went further, with a rule that any player who lowers his head to initiate contact with his helmet can be penalized. The spirit of both rules is the same. Stop players from using the top of their head as a weapon. And that is a good goal.

But here is the problem. In real time, at full speed, the difference between a runner dropping his shoulder and a runner dropping his helmet can be a matter of inches and fractions of a second. Officials have to make that call instantly, from one angle, with bodies flying everywhere. That is exactly where the gray area lives. And it is why Harbaugh wants clearer direction. I think that is a completely fair question, and I bet a lot of coaches around the league have asked it.

Now, I want to be careful here, because the NFL’s rules about helmet contact exist for a reason. Player safety matters, and it should. Nobody wants to see a defender or a running back get hurt because of a dangerous blow to the head. Harbaugh is not arguing that safety should be ignored. He is arguing that coaches need guidance they can actually teach. If the standard is not clear, you cannot coach your players to stay on the right side of it. And that is a reasonable thing to ask for.

And the stakes for the Giants are real. A running back can win the collision and still lose the down if a flag comes out. A 15-yard penalty can wipe out a big run, put the offense in a terrible spot, and swing the momentum of a game. On the road, in a division game, against a desperate team, one of those flags at the wrong moment could be the difference between a win and a loss.

But now look at what the Giants would be asking Skattebo to change. Against Houston, he ran for 124 yards and a touchdown on 17 carries. That is more than seven yards per carry, against a Texans run defense that came in ranked third in the league. It was his first 100-yard game as a pro. He scored on a 16-yard run late in regulation, and he broke off a 42-yard run in overtime to set up Devin Singletary’s game-winning touchdown. That production is not an accident. It comes from his aggression, his vision, and his refusal to go down easy. Every hard run sets up easier third downs. Every defender who has to commit to stopping him opens something up for the passing game. And every physical drive changes the energy on the Giants’ sideline.

So, when Skattebo says he is not changing, I understand it. That style is who he is. One analyst called him a 1988 running back, a guy who goes looking for contact instead of running away from it. Harbaugh said he played with a fierce joy in Houston, not a happy joy, a fierce joy, and that is what football is. If you take that away from him, you might take away the very thing that makes him special. And honestly, I do not want him to become a softer runner. That style is a big reason this offense found an identity in Houston.

And remember where Skattebo came from. He was a fourth-round pick out of Arizona State in 2025, a back who was known in college for being almost impossible to bring down. Scouting departments did not all agree on him, because he was not the fastest back in his class and he did not have the shiftiness of the elite runners. But what he had was something you cannot teach. He makes defenders pay for every tackle. That is how a fourth-round pick turns into the emotional engine of an NFL offense. You do not ask a player like that to run like somebody else.

And think about what his running does for J.J. McCarthy. When a defense has to commit an extra defender to the box to stop a back like Skattebo, it opens up the play-action passing game. Safeties creep up, linebackers bite on the fake, and suddenly there is space behind them for Malik Nabers, Isaiah Likely, and Malachi Fields. A young quarterback in his second start, on the road in Philadelphia, would love nothing more than to have that kind of run game making his life easier. Skattebo’s physicality is not just about Skattebo. It is the foundation the whole offense is built on.

And I respect the way Harbaugh handled this. He has coached long enough to know how much a player’s confidence matters. During his years in Baltimore, his teams were built on physical, downhill football, and he understands that a running back who starts hesitating at contact loses what makes him effective. A back who is thinking about a flag on every carry is a back who runs half a step slower. And Harbaugh has coached the other kind of power back too. In 2024, he had Derrick Henry in Baltimore, one of the most punishing runners of his generation. Henry runs with a forward lean, a brutal stiff-arm, and his pads low, but he punishes defenders with his body, not the crown of his helmet. Harbaugh has seen up close that a back can be violent and still stay within the rules. That is probably the model he has in his head for Skattebo. So, defending Skattebo publicly is as much about protecting that mentality as it is about one call.

And there is another layer to this. When a coach publicly defends his player, the rest of the locker room notices. It tells them their coach will stand up for them and explain their intentions, instead of throwing them under the bus. That builds trust. And remember the history between these two. Two weeks ago, Harbaugh gave Skattebo zero carries against the Saints, after his overtime fumble in Washington. That was tough love. Now, after Skattebo answered with the best game of his career, Harbaugh is in front of the cameras defending him. That is a coach who is hard on his players when they make mistakes and loyal to them when they compete the right way. Players run through walls for coaches like that.

But here is the honest part. Trust only matters if it turns into smarter football. The real test is not a press conference. It is the next game. And Skattebo’s decision comes with real risks that I want to lay out.

First, the flags. If officials have flagged him once for this, they will be watching for it again. Every time he lowers his pads near the sideline or in the open field, there is a chance a flag comes out. That is 15 yards each time, and those yards can kill drives.

Second, the fines. Skattebo said he would play for free if that is what it takes. That is a great line. But fines add up, and more importantly, repeated flags can start to shape how officials view a player. You do not want to become known as the guy who gets flagged for this.

Third, his own health. A running back who lowers his helmet into contact over and over is putting his own head and neck at risk, not just the defender’s. The rules exist partly to protect the ball carrier too. Skattebo plays with no fear, and I love that. But the Giants need him healthy for the next ten games, not just the next one.

And fourth, ball security. According to ESPN, Skattebo fumbled five times in his first 13 career games. His overtime fumble in Washington turned a likely win into a tie. The physical style that makes him great also puts the football at risk when defenders are punching at it in a pile. Harbaugh’s message all season has been that ball security is the most important thing with this offense. Skattebo answered in Houston, with zero fumbles. He has to keep answering.

So, what would I tell him? Keep running the way you run. Do not become a different player. But maybe make a small adjustment, especially on plays near the sideline or in open space, where the crown-of-the-helmet rule is more likely to draw a flag. Inside the tackle box, in a pile at the line of scrimmage, you have more room to run with your pads low. Out in the open, win with the shoulder. Keep your eyes up. Drop the shoulder, not the crown. It is not about becoming soft. It is about staying on the field and keeping your yards.

And Giants fans have seen backs like this before. Brandon Jacobs built a career running through defensive backs, a 260-pound back who made safeties think twice about trying to tackle him. He played with that same fierce, physical style, and he was a huge part of two Super Bowl teams. The great physical backs find a way to keep their edge while staying within the rules. That is the next step for Skattebo.

And there is a second concern, and this one might be bigger. His hamstring. Late in the Houston game, Skattebo said he felt his hamstring grab on his biggest run of the day, that 42-yarder in overtime. Hamstring issues are tricky for running backs. They affect burst, balance, and the ability to accelerate through narrow holes, which is exactly what makes Skattebo dangerous. The good news is that the bye gives the coaching staff time to evaluate him before the Giants visit Philadelphia on November 8th. But the Giants need him fully himself for that game, not a version of himself limited by a lingering injury.

And his health matters even more right now because of the rest of the backfield. Tyrone Tracy Jr. is on injured reserve and will miss at least the next four games. That means the Giants are thinner at running back than they have been all season. Devin Singletary is the backup. An undrafted rookie off the Chargers’ practice squad is the third back. And Patrick Ricard is the fullback. If Skattebo’s hamstring lingers, the offense loses its identity. That is why I expect the staff to be cautious with him in practice, even if he says he feels fine. The bye is a gift in that sense. Two weeks to get the hamstring right without missing a game.

Now, quickly, two other stories from this week that connect to Skattebo, because they are all part of the same question. Can the Giants combine toughness with discipline?

First, the defense has its own fierce-joy player, and it is rookie linebacker Arvell Reese. Seven games into his career, Reese keeps getting more responsibility and keeps answering the call. He thinks fast, he plays fast, and he hits hard, with 56 tackles, one and a half sacks, and an interception. With Tremaine Edmunds out for the season, Reese’s relentless style is starting to shape the personality of the defense. There is already early talk about him as a Defensive Rookie of the Year candidate. I want to keep that in perspective, because awards are not handed out in October. But when your best rookie is also setting a demanding standard for effort, his value goes beyond the box score.

Second, the Giants signed veteran defensive tackle Dean Lowry to the practice squad, after preliminary talks back in September that did not lead to a deal. Lowry was a fourth-round pick by Green Bay in 2016, spent seven seasons with the Packers, became a regular starter, and recorded a career-high five sacks in 2021. He later spent time with Minnesota and Pittsburgh. At 6 foot 6 and around 296 pounds, he brings length and size. But the important question is not what he did five years ago. It is what he can do now. He tore his ACL in training camp in 2025 and missed that entire season, and Pittsburgh released him during final cuts this year. So, nobody should pretend this signing guarantees anything. His realistic value is experienced, disciplined depth, a player who holds his gap on first down instead of chasing a highlight. And that discipline in the trenches is exactly what keeps Reese free to run to the football.

So, let me connect all three stories. Skattebo wants to keep running through defenders without giving away yards to penalties. Reese is giving the Giants a glimpse of their defensive future with relentless effort. And Lowry is competing for a depth role that can matter more than people expect. Every one of these stories asks the same question. Can the Giants be tough and smart at the same time?

Because Philadelphia will not care about headlines. The Eagles will test what the Giants can actually execute. A division game on the road can expose careless penalties, poor tackling, and weak blocking faster than any scouting report can hide them. And the Eagles, as banged up as they are without Jalen Hurts, are still dangerous at home. They have been stuck in a losing streak, and they just lost 38 to 21 to Dallas on Monday night. A desperate team in front of its own crowd is a very different opponent. The Giants have to treat that game like it is against the best version of the Eagles, not the one on the losing streak.

And this is where Skattebo’s decision really matters. In a game like that, physicality wins. The team that runs harder, hits harder, and wins the collisions usually wins in November. Skattebo’s style is exactly what the Giants need in Philadelphia. But one bad flag on a big third down could flip the game. That is the balance he has to find. Run like Skattebo. Just do it with your eyes up.

And think about the time of year. November football in Philadelphia is cold, physical, and loud. The fields get harder, the hits get heavier, and the teams that can run the ball and stop the run usually come out on top. That is exactly the kind of football Skattebo was built for. If his hamstring is right, he could be the most important player on the field in that game.

Here is what I will be watching. Number one, Skattebo’s hamstring and whether he practices fully coming out of the bye. Number two, whether the league responds to Harbaugh’s request for clarity, or whether this stays a gray area. Number three, how many carries the backup backs get in Philadelphia, because that tells you how much the coaches trust the hamstring. And number four, the penalty column. Zero personal fouls has to be the goal.

The Giants are 4 and 2 and 1, in first place in the NFC East, with a running back who refuses to change and a head coach who has his back. That is a combination I love. Toughness, loyalty, and a whole lot of fierce joy. Now they just have to add the discipline. I have said it all season, and I am saying it again. This team can go all the way.
