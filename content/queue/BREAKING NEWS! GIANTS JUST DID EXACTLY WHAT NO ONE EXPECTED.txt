Two hundred and thirty. That is how many straight games the New York Giants had lost since 1970 when trailing by 14 or more points in the final eight minutes of regulation. Two hundred and thirty times in a row, folks. More than half a century of the same ending. And on Sunday in Washington, the Giants trailed the Commanders 27 to 13 with eleven minutes left. By every bit of history this franchise has, that game was over. And they still walked off that field without a loss, alone in first place in the NFC East. Along the way, a tight end did something no Giants tight end had ever done in the history of this franchise. A quarterback did something no Giant had done since Eli Manning. And a receiver who asked for the football got it twenty times. Today I want to tell you the calm, honest story of how this team keeps surviving, the price it has paid to get here, the records that fell in Washington, and why you should feel genuinely good about where this season is headed. Let’s get into it.

Chapter one of this story is the math, because the math was supposed to end this season back in September. After the week two loss to the Rams, ESPN’s Football Power Index dropped the Giants’ playoff chances from 43 percent all the way down to 1 percent. One percent, folks. The same model projected a five-win season. Five wins. Now look at where we are three weeks later. Three wins, one loss, one tie, and alone on top of the NFC East. A tie counts as half a win in the standings, which gives this team a winning percentage of 70 percent through five games. Keep that pace over a full season and you are looking at roughly twelve wins. From one percent to first place. That is not a rebuild, folks. That is a resurrection.

But I promised you the honest version, so let me tell you how the record lies a little, in both directions. On Sunday in Washington, the Giants gained just 77 yards in the entire first half. Their running backs carried the ball eleven times for 21 yards before halftime. The offense lost two fumbles. The kicking game left points on the field. And the defense let a mobile quarterback escape for big runs. Folks, a team that makes that many mistakes usually loses by double digits. This one did not lose at all. And that says plenty about this roster.

Now here is the counterfactual that should calm every Giants fan down. If one more field goal goes through the uprights, the Giants walk out of Washington 4 and 1. If Cam Skattebo keeps the ball tucked on one overtime carry, the same thing happens. This team came within a single snap of a two-game lead over everybody in the division, while playing its worst half of the season. Think about what that means. The floor of this team, on a bad day, on the road, in the rain, against a division rival with its franchise quarterback back, is a tie. That is a high floor. John Harbaugh summed it up perfectly after the game. He said, I am happy it was not a loss, and I am sad it was not a win. And folks, that honest middle ground is exactly where this season lives right now.

And to understand how we got here, you have to go back to the beginning. The season opened under the lights on Sunday Night Football, with a statement. The Giants beat the Dallas Cowboys 28 to 20 in John Harbaugh’s debut, his first game as Giants head coach after eighteen years in Baltimore. Jackson Dart completed 23 of 29 passes for 230 yards and three touchdowns. For one week, the whole league was talking about a young quarterback and a new coach building something real in New Jersey.

Then came week two in Los Angeles. Dart took a hit from both sides on the opening drive, his left knee bent the wrong way, and the offense never recovered. The Giants lost 28 to 6 to the Rams. That was the night the FPI dropped them to one percent, and the night a lot of people wrote the season off.

Week three showed what this team looks like when nothing comes easy. A nor’easter at MetLife Stadium. The Giants beat the Tennessee Titans 12 to 7 without scoring a single touchdown, and Jevon Holland intercepted Cam Ward with 17 seconds left to seal it. And Jameis Winston said something afterward that I think about all the time. Today is the only day that we have, and today it was enough.

Week four brought an even wilder finish against Arizona. With the Giants clinging to a six-point lead, Deonte Banks intercepted Jacoby Brissett on fourth and goal and ran 98 yards for a touchdown as time expired, sealing a 36 to 24 win.

And then came Washington. Jayden Daniels came back from a dislocated left elbow and ran straight through the middle of the Giants defense for touchdowns of 38 and 26 yards, finishing with 103 rushing yards. The Giants trailed by 14 with eleven minutes left. And what followed was the third straight week where the final seconds decided everything. And for the third straight week, the Giants were still standing at the end. Three late finishes in a row, folks. That tells you something real about a locker room.

And let me put that 230 number in perspective one more time, because I do not think people realize how wild it is. That streak, reported by the New York Post, covers every Giants team since 1970. The Parcells teams. The Coughlin teams. The two Super Bowl runs with Eli Manning. Lawrence Taylor, Phil Simms, Michael Strahan, all of them. None of those teams ever came back from 14 or more points down in the final eight minutes of regulation and avoided a loss. Not once in 230 tries. And this team, with a backup quarterback, a banged-up roster, and a rookie-heavy defense, finally did it. Folks, you do not have to win a Super Bowl to make franchise history. Sometimes you just have to refuse to lose on a rainy Sunday in Washington.

Chapter two is the price this roster has already paid just to get here, because the list is long and it hurts. Jackson Dart had surgery to repair the meniscus and the MCL in his left knee. As Dan Duggan of The Athletic reported, his ACL stayed intact. His regular season is over, and even a playoff return remains unclear. But that ACL detail matters a lot for the long run, folks. It means the young quarterback the Giants built this offense around is expected to make a full recovery and return as the face of the franchise next season. The future is safe.

One week later, the defense lost its leader. Brian Burns tore the ACL and the lateral meniscus in his right knee against Tennessee. Burns finished second in the NFL with a career-high 16 and a half sacks last season, behind only Myles Garrett. Duggan noted that the typical recovery from that surgery runs nine to twelve months, which points toward a return for the start of next season. Losing a starting quarterback and a Pro Bowl edge rusher in back-to-back weeks breaks most teams in this league. It did not break this one. And that response says a lot about the culture Harbaugh is building.

And Sunday added more names to the list. Right tackle Jermaine Eluemunor left with a calf injury. Safety Tyler Nubin left with a neck injury. Rookie cornerback Colton Hood left with a concussion. And safety Jason Pinnock was already inactive. Let me be fair here, because no team in this league has enough depth to absorb all of that without bending. But what stands out is how the coaches answered. In the second half, they scrapped the run-first plan, went to a no-huddle, drop-back attack, and handed the game to a 32-year-old veteran quarterback. And that is where the comeback started.

Which brings us to chapter three, the arrivals worth celebrating. And the first one is Jameis Winston. In the first half on Sunday, Winston went 7 of 16 for 72 yards. It was ugly, folks. But once the offense spread out and sped up, he completed 26 of 38 passes for 254 yards across the second half and overtime. He finished with 326 yards, three touchdowns, and zero interceptions. And according to the Giants, he became the first Giant with back-to-back three-touchdown games since Eli Manning in 2016. Think about that. The last Giants quarterback to do that is a two-time Super Bowl MVP. Winston has now thrown six touchdown passes in his last two starts, and this offense has finally found an identity. When the Giants go no-huddle and let Jameis sling it, defenses cannot keep up.

The second arrival is Isaiah Likely, who followed Harbaugh from Baltimore on a three-year deal worth 40 million dollars. And on Sunday, he was unstoppable. Eleven catches for 94 yards and two touchdowns, including a three-yard score and a 17-yard score that was first ruled a fumble and then reversed on review. According to the team, Likely became the first tight end in franchise history with at least nine catches and two touchdown catches in a single game. Folks, this franchise has had some great tight ends. Mark Bavaro. Jeremy Shockey. Not one of them ever did that. Isaiah Likely did it in his fifth game as a Giant. And he is the reason the middle of the field kept opening up every time Washington blitzed. When defenses send pressure, Likely is the safety valve that turns their aggression into first downs.

And then add Malik Nabers to the picture. Back from a torn ACL, he caught six passes for 112 yards against Arizona. Then against Washington, he caught eleven passes for 121 yards and the game-tying touchdown, on twenty targets. Twenty targets, folks. After the Arizona game, Nabers said he wanted the football more. Harbaugh and Matt Nagy heard him, and Winston fed him. And here is the history. Nabers and Likely became just the sixth pair of teammates in league history with at least eleven catches, 90 yards, and a touchdown each in the same game. And only the second receiver and tight end pair to do it, after Justin Jefferson and T.J. Hockenson. That is the company these two are keeping now.

And remember, Nabers was dealing with knee soreness last week and missed Wednesday’s practice. He came back as limited on Thursday, and then went out and got twenty targets on the road. That tells you everything about his toughness and his trust in his body after that ACL surgery.

And folks, I want to spend one more minute on that no-huddle switch, because I think it might be the most important development of the entire game. For five weeks, this offense has tried to be a power running team. Heavy formations, a fullback, Skattebo and Harris pounding away. And it worked beautifully in week one against Dallas. But since then, the run game has struggled, and in Washington, it hit rock bottom with 21 yards on eleven carries in the first half. So the coaches did something a lot of stubborn staffs never do. They adapted in the middle of a game. They spread the field, sped up the tempo, and let their best players attack. Nabers outside, Likely in the middle, Winston throwing on rhythm. And Washington could not adjust. The Commanders were blitzing, and every time they brought pressure, Likely was sitting in the space they vacated. That is how you turn a 77-yard first half into a 254-yard second half and overtime from your quarterback alone.

Now, does that mean the Giants should abandon the run and go no-huddle every week? No. Balance still matters, especially in December when the weather turns. But it means the Giants now have a second identity they can switch to when the first one stalls. A power team that can turn into a spread, up-tempo team at halftime is very hard to prepare for. Opposing coordinators now have to study both versions. And against a Saints defense coming to MetLife this week, I would not be surprised to see the Giants come out with a lot more tempo early, to set the tone before New Orleans can settle in.

The third arrival is J.J. McCarthy, who came over from Minnesota for a 2027 fifth-round pick. His Giants debut in Washington was quiet, and that is fine. His story here is far from finished. With an A-plus grade from Matt Nagy for learning the offense, a spot as the official backup, and a package of plays the coaches can call whenever they want, McCarthy is exactly where a young quarterback should be. Learning, developing, and waiting for his moment while the veteran in front of him is playing the best football of his Giants career.

Chapter four is the draft class, and this one has already changed the defense. The Giants made seven picks in Harbaugh’s first draft, and the top of it was bold. They took Arvell Reese at number five and Francis Mauigoa at number ten. And Reese is already playing like a top-five pick. He was named Pepsi Rookie of the Week, he became the youngest Giants player of the Super Bowl era to record an interception, and in Washington, he added nine tackles plus his first career sack. And here is the stat that blew me away. According to the Giants, Reese is the only Giant in the last 30 seasons to reach 40 tackles through the first five games of his career. Thirty seasons, folks. Think about all the linebackers who have come through this building in that time. None of them started like this. Reese is not just a good rookie. He is on a historic pace.

And Mauigoa, the massive lineman out of Miami, has been lining up with the starting offensive line as a rookie. Everybody laughed at the idea of drafting a lineman that high in the first round. Nobody is laughing now. And with Jermaine Eluemunor hurt, Mauigoa’s versatility up front might become even more important in the weeks ahead.

Chapter five is the defense as a whole, because I want to be honest about it. This defense entered Sunday with the fewest sacks in the NFL. That is a real problem, and it showed against a mobile quarterback like Daniels. But look closer and you see the foundation of something good. Abdul Carter keeps winning his matchups, posting one of the best pass rush grades in the league against Arizona even without a sack. Reese is stacking tackles like a veteran and just got his first sack. Jadeveon Clowney adds a veteran edge with Baltimore roots. Daron Payne anchors the interior. And here is the stat I keep coming back to. With the game on the line three weeks in a row, this defense has stood its ground every single time. Holland’s interception against Tennessee. Banks’ pick six against Arizona. And in Washington, even after Skattebo’s fumble handed the Commanders the ball in overtime, this team was still standing when the clock hit zero. Sacks will come. Clutch is already here.

So now let’s talk about the real question, and it is not about the tie itself. It is about one line in the NFC East standings and what it means for the next twelve games. The Giants are 3 and 1 and 1. The Eagles are 2 and 3. The Cowboys are 2 and 3. The Commanders are 1 and 3 and 1. The Giants are a game and a half clear of everybody, and they are the only team in the division with a winning record. A team that the analytics gave a one percent chance of making the playoffs three weeks ago is now the favorite in its division. And the schedule ahead gives them a chance to build on it. A home game against the New Orleans Saints, Winston’s old team, at MetLife, where the Giants have not lost all season. Then a road trip to Houston. Then the week eight bye. Get to the bye at 5 and 1 and 1, and the Giants will have a real cushion heading into the second half of the season.

And here is why you should feel good, folks, even after a tie. This team has proven it can win ugly, like Tennessee. It can win wild, like Arizona. And it can survive a terrible half and still come back from fourteen down late on the road, something this franchise had failed to do two hundred and thirty straight times. The quarterback is hot. The tight end just made history. The receiver is back to being a star. The rookie linebacker is on a historic pace. And the head coach keeps telling his players the same thing, win the next game. That is the message. Win the next game. It is simple, it is honest, and it is working.

And let me say this clearly, because I believe it more every week. This team can go all the way this season. Not because everything has gone right, but because so much has gone wrong and they keep finding ways to survive. Teams like that are dangerous in January. Teams like that do not fold under pressure, because they have been living under pressure since September.

That is going to do it for this one, family.
